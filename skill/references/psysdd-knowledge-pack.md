# PSYSDD Knowledge Pack

_A single, self-contained reference for the PSYSDD psychiatric differential-diagnosis framework, intended for upload as knowledge to a Claude Project (or any assistant). Generated from the versioned data in `/data` — do not edit by hand._

**How an assistant should use this pack for a case**
1. Apply the **six-step method** below in order — rule out feigning, substances, and a medical cause before pursuing a primary disorder.
2. Choose the **decision tree** matching the presenting symptom and reason through its decision points using the case facts, stating YES/NO and the reason at each point.
3. On reaching a candidate diagnosis, open that disorder's **differential table** and show how the main competitors are excluded.
4. State what additional history, exam, or investigations would change the conclusion, and note that the **full DSM-5 criteria** must be confirmed. Never present output as a definitive diagnosis.

> **For clinician reference and research/educational use only — not a diagnostic substitute.** This pack summarizes differentiating features only; it omits full criteria, durations, and severity thresholds. It does not diagnose and does not replace clinical judgment or the full DSM-5 text.

## Part 1 — The Six-Step Differential Diagnosis Method

Before working through a symptom decision tree, orient every case with this ordered method. The steps move from the most fundamental questions about the validity and etiology of the presentation toward the specific primary disorder and, finally, the boundary with normality. Each step should be considered in order because an earlier step can render later ones moot (for example, a substance etiology reframes the entire differential).

### Step 1: Rule out feigning (Malingering and Factitious Disorder)

**Confirm the symptoms are reported in good faith before anything else — if the presentation is fabricated or exaggerated, no downstream diagnosis is reliable.**

Malingering and Factitious Disorder both involve intentionally produced or feigned symptoms; they are separated by motivation. Malingering is driven by an external, tangible incentive (compensation, avoiding work or legal consequences, obtaining medications or shelter). In Factitious Disorder the deception continues even without an obvious external reward. Raise suspicion when there is a clear secondary gain, when the picture matches a lay stereotype of illness rather than a recognized syndrome, when the story shifts markedly between encounters, when the presentation mirrors a role model, or when the patient is notably manipulative or suggestible. This step is context-dependent — it matters most in forensic, disability, and correctional settings — which is why it is not repeated inside every decision tree.

Raise suspicion when:
- Clear external incentive tied to being diagnosed (disability, forensic, prison)
- Symptom cluster resembles a lay notion of mental illness more than a real syndrome
- Presentation changes radically between encounters
- Presentation mimics a nearby role model (another patient, an ill relative)
- Patient is characteristically manipulative or highly suggestible

### Step 2: Rule out a substance / medication etiology

**Ask first whether a substance, medication, or toxin acting on the CNS is producing the symptoms — the single most commonly missed cause in practice.**

Almost any psychiatric presentation can be produced by a substance. First establish whether the person is using anything (history, collateral, exam, and toxicology, since intake is routinely underestimated). Then decide the relationship: (a) the substance directly causes the symptoms → a Substance/Medication-Induced Disorder, Intoxication, or Withdrawal; (b) the substance use is a consequence or associated feature of a primary disorder (e.g., self-medication); or (c) the two are independent. Attribute symptoms to a substance when there is a plausible temporal link, when the amount and duration are sufficient to cause that symptom, and when no better non-substance explanation exists. Persistence well beyond intoxication/withdrawal (roughly one month of abstinence is the DSM-5 guideline, applied flexibly) points toward a primary disorder — with the noted exceptions of substance-induced neurocognitive disorder and hallucinogen persisting perception disorder.

Raise suspicion when:
- Is the person using any drug of abuse, medication, or toxin?
- Is there a close temporal relationship between use and symptom onset/maintenance?
- Is the amount and duration of use sufficient to cause this symptom?
- Do symptoms persist during a sustained period of abstinence (points to primary disorder)?
- Is there a strong alternative explanation (family history, prior episodes off substances)?

### Step 3: Rule out a disorder due to another medical condition

**Determine whether a nonpsychiatric medical condition is directly producing the symptoms via its physiological effect on the brain.**

Virtually any presentation can be the direct physiological consequence of a general medical condition (e.g., depressive disorder due to hypothyroidism, psychosis due to a CNS lesion). Two clues raise the index of suspicion: (1) a temporal pattern in which the psychiatric symptoms follow the onset of the medical condition, vary with its severity, and remit when it resolves; and (2) an atypical presentation — unusual age at onset, atypical symptom pattern, or atypical course (e.g., first manic episode in an elderly patient, severe cognitive or weight loss out of proportion to a mild mood disturbance). Neither clue is infallible; symptoms can be the first harbinger of a medical illness or a late complication. Direct the workup toward conditions that are both common and plausible for the presenting symptom rather than screening indiscriminately.

Raise suspicion when:
- Do symptoms begin after, track with, and remit alongside a medical condition?
- Is the presentation atypical in symptom pattern, age at onset, or course?
- Are there physical exam or laboratory findings pointing to an organic cause?
- Could a medication for the medical condition be responsible (loops back to Step 2)?

### Step 4: Determine the specific primary disorder(s)

**With substances and medical causes excluded, identify which primary DSM-5 disorder(s) best account for the presentation.**

This is where the symptom decision trees do most of their work. DSM-5 groups disorders around shared presenting symptoms precisely to structure this differential. Follow the relevant tree from the presenting symptom through its decision points. Because comorbidity is common and diagnoses are frequently not mutually exclusive, more than one tree may apply, and a single tree may need to be traversed more than once to capture co-occurring conditions. After reaching a candidate diagnosis, review its differential table (Chapter 3) to confirm the main competitors have been considered and ruled out.

### Step 5: Differentiate Adjustment Disorder from residual Other Specified / Unspecified categories

**For clinically significant presentations that don't meet a specific disorder's threshold, decide between an Adjustment Disorder and a residual category.**

Many real presentations fall below the symptom count, severity, or duration required for a specific diagnosis yet still cause clinically significant distress or impairment and reflect a genuine dysfunction. If the symptoms are a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor, diagnose an Adjustment Disorder — the key judgment is whether the stressor is etiological, not merely present. Otherwise, use the Other Specified or Unspecified category of the grouping that best fits the symptoms. Choose Other Specified when you wish to record the specific reason the presentation falls short; choose Unspecified when you decline to specify (insufficient information, or a clinical reason not to).

### Step 6: Establish the boundary with no mental disorder

**Confirm the presentation actually crosses the threshold into disorder — clinically significant distress or impairment reflecting an internal dysfunction.**

Most DSM-5 criteria sets require that the disturbance cause clinically significant distress or impairment in functioning. Many individual symptoms (transient anxiety, low mood, insomnia, sexual difficulty) are part of ordinary experience and are not, by themselves, a disorder. 'Clinically significant' is deliberately left to clinical judgment and is shaped by culture, setting, and available resources; in help-seeking clinical populations the threshold is usually already met. Finally, some impairing but normative reactions (e.g., uncomplicated bereavement) do not represent an internal dysfunction and are coded, if at all, under conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention rather than as a mental disorder.

**On comorbidity.** Differential diagnosis usually means choosing one best explanation from mutually exclusive contenders, but DSM-5 diagnoses are frequently not mutually exclusive. Assigning more than one diagnosis is often necessary to describe a presentation fully, and comorbid conditions can relate to one another in several ways (one causing another, a shared underlying cause, artificial splitting of one syndrome, definitional overlap, or chance co-occurrence). Multiple diagnoses do not imply multiple independent disease processes — treat DSM-5 diagnoses as descriptive building blocks for communication.

### Tree 2.1 — Poor School Performance

_Presenting: Poor school performance · Group: Neurodevelopmental presentations_

Poor academic performance is common and nonspecific. Most childhood mental disorders can impair it, and it is frequently the chief complaint — but it is not itself a disorder. Evaluation typically pairs cognitive/achievement testing with a careful history from parents, teachers, and clinicians. Because neurodevelopmental and other disorders often co-occur, traverse the tree more than once and assign every applicable diagnosis.

- **Q: Are there deficits in intellectual function plus deficits in adaptive functioning, with onset during the developmental period?**
  - YES → Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder) — Intellectual Disability (differential Table 3.1.1)
  - NO → next question: Are there deficits in the social use of verbal and nonverbal communication?
- **Q: Are there deficits in the social use of verbal and nonverbal communication?**
  - _If accompanied by restricted/repetitive behavior and deficits in relationships and social-emotional reciprocity, consider Autism Spectrum Disorder; otherwise Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder._
  - YES → next question: Are there also restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior/interests and deficits in developing/understanding relationships?
  - NO → next question: Is there difficulty learning and using specific academic skills (reading, writing, mathematics)?
- **Q: Are there also restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior/interests and deficits in developing/understanding relationships?**
  - YES → Autism Spectrum Disorder — Autism Spectrum Disorder (differential Table 3.1.3)
  - NO → Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder — Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder (differential Table 3.1.2)
- **Q: Is there difficulty learning and using specific academic skills (reading, writing, mathematics)?**

  - NO → next question: Are there persistent difficulties in the acquisition and use of language?
- **Q: Are there persistent difficulties in the acquisition and use of language?**

  - YES → Selective Mutism — Selective Mutism (differential Table 3.5.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, onset before age 12, interfering with functioning across settings?

  - NO → next question: Is there a pattern of antisocial behavior (e.g., truancy, rule violations)?
- **Q: Is there a pattern of antisocial behavior (e.g., truancy, rule violations)?**
  - YES → Conduct Disorder — Conduct Disorder (differential Table 3.14.3)
  - NO → next question: Is there a pattern of negativistic, hostile, and defiant behavior?
- **Q: Is there a pattern of negativistic, hostile, and defiant behavior?**
  - YES → Oppositional Defiant Disorder — Oppositional Defiant Disorder (differential Table 3.14.1)
  - NO → next question: Is the poor performance related to excessive substance use?
- **Q: Is the poor performance related to excessive substance use?**
  - YES → Substance Use Disorder — Substance Use Disorder (differential Table 3.15.1) (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is there school refusal based on fear of separation from attachment figures?
- **Q: Is there school refusal based on fear of separation from attachment figures?**
  - YES → Separation Anxiety Disorder — Separation Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.1)
  - NO → next question: Is another anxiety, mood, or psychotic disorder interfering with school performance?
- **Q: Is another anxiety, mood, or psychotic disorder interfering with school performance?**
  - YES → Indicate the specific disorder — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1); Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor?

### Tree 2.2 — Behavioral Problems in a Child or Adolescent

_Presenting: Behavioral problems in a child or adolescent · Group: Neurodevelopmental presentations_

Many behavioral problems in youth are not due to a mental disorder — some are subthreshold, some reflect family dysfunction, and some serious acts (e.g., for financial gain) fall outside DSM-5. Early-childhood onset points toward ADHD, ODD, DMDD, ASD, or intellectual disability; adolescent onset raises the role of substances and adolescent-onset conduct disorder, mood disorders, and psychosis.

- **Q: Are the behaviors associated with substance use (including medication)?**

- **Q: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**

- **Q: Do severe temper outbursts occur that are grossly out of proportion, with persistent anger/irritability between them?**
  - YES → Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder — Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (differential Table 3.4.4)
  - NO → next question: Is there a persistent pattern of hyperactivity, impulsivity, and inattention across two or more settings with onset before age 12?
- **Q: Is there a persistent pattern of hyperactivity, impulsivity, and inattention across two or more settings with onset before age 12?**
  - YES → Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (differential Table 3.1.4)
  - NO → next question: Is there a pattern of argumentativeness, defiance, and vindictiveness beyond developmental norms?
- **Q: Is there a pattern of argumentativeness, defiance, and vindictiveness beyond developmental norms?**
  - YES → Oppositional Defiant Disorder — Oppositional Defiant Disorder (differential Table 3.14.1)
  - NO → next question: Do the behaviors occur with intellectual and adaptive deficits with onset in the developmental period?
- **Q: Do the behaviors occur with intellectual and adaptive deficits with onset in the developmental period?**
  - YES → Intellectual Disability — Intellectual Disability (differential Table 3.1.1)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur with persistent deficits in social communication/interaction plus restricted, repetitive behaviors?
- **Q: Do they occur with persistent deficits in social communication/interaction plus restricted, repetitive behaviors?**
  - YES → Autism Spectrum Disorder — Autism Spectrum Disorder (differential Table 3.1.3)
  - NO → next question: Are they a consequence of stereotyped, repetitive motor movements?
- **Q: Are they a consequence of stereotyped, repetitive motor movements?**
  - YES → Stereotypic Movement Disorder — Stereotypic Movement Disorder
  - NO → next question: Are they part of a repetitive, persistent pattern of antisocial behavior violating others' rights or major norms?
- **Q: Are they part of a repetitive, persistent pattern of antisocial behavior violating others' rights or major norms?**
  - YES → Conduct Disorder — Conduct Disorder (differential Table 3.14.3)
  - NO → next question: Is there deliberate, purposeful fire setting associated with arousal/fascination?
- **Q: Is there deliberate, purposeful fire setting associated with arousal/fascination?**
  - YES → Pyromania — Pyromania
  - NO → next question: Is there recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects not needed for use or monetary value?
- **Q: Is there recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects not needed for use or monetary value?**
  - YES → Kleptomania — Kleptomania
  - NO → next question: Are the behaviors associated with periods of elevated, euphoric, or irritable mood plus increased energy?
- **Q: Are the behaviors associated with periods of elevated, euphoric, or irritable mood plus increased energy?**
  - YES → Manic/Hypomanic Episode — Bipolar I Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1); Bipolar II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.2); Cyclothymic Disorder (differential Table 3.3.3)
  - NO → next question: Are they associated with episodes of depressed/irritable mood plus other depressive symptoms?
- **Q: Are they associated with episodes of depressed/irritable mood plus other depressive symptoms?**
  - YES → Major Depressive Episode — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1); Persistent Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.2)
  - NO → next question: Are they associated with psychotic symptoms?
- **Q: Are they associated with psychotic symptoms?**
  - YES → A psychotic disorder is present — Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Do the problems occur as a symptomatic response to a psychosocial stressor of an extremely traumatic nature, with re-experiencing?
- **Q: Do the problems occur as a symptomatic response to a psychosocial stressor of an extremely traumatic nature, with re-experiencing?**
  - YES → PTSD or Acute Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder / Acute Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1)
  - NO → next question: Are they a maladaptive response to a (non-traumatic) psychosocial stressor?
- **Q: Are they a maladaptive response to a (non-traumatic) psychosocial stressor?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → next question: Are the problems clinically significant and representative of a psychological/biological dysfunction, but not covered above?
- **Q: Are the problems clinically significant and representative of a psychological/biological dysfunction, but not covered above?**
  - YES → Residual category — Other Specified / Unspecified Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorder
  - NO → next question: Is the behavior illegal behavior for gain or revenge (not a dysfunction in the individual)?

  - NO → Age-appropriate rambunctious behavior — not a mental disorder

### Tree 2.3 — Speech Disturbance

_Presenting: Disorganized, impaired, or unusual speech · Group: Neurodevelopmental presentations_

Covers disorganized speech (derailment, tangentiality), impaired speech production (language, articulation, fluency), and unusual speech (pragmatic deficits, pressured/repetitive speech). Disorganized speech is hard to judge — if you cannot readily tell whether speech is disorganized, it probably is not pathological. Distinguishing loosening of associations (schizophrenia) from flight of ideas (mania) is best based on accompanying symptoms and course rather than the speech pattern alone.

- **Q: Is the speech disturbance associated with a fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness?**
  - YES → Delirium — Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it associated with decline in one or more cognitive domains (attention, executive function, memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition)?
- **Q: Is it associated with decline in one or more cognitive domains (attention, executive function, memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition)?**
  - YES → Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (or aphasia) — Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication)?**
  - YES → Substance-induced — Substance Intoxication/Withdrawal or Substance-Induced Delirium (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., aphasia)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., aphasia)?**
  - YES → Medical etiology — Speech disturbance due to another medical condition (e.g., aphasia) (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is there rapid, pressured speech with racing thoughts, euphoric/irritable mood, and increased energy?
- **Q: Is there rapid, pressured speech with racing thoughts, euphoric/irritable mood, and increased energy?**
  - YES → Manic/Hypomanic Episode — Bipolar I/II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Is speech disorganized (derailment, incoherence) in the context of other psychotic symptoms?
- **Q: Is speech disorganized (derailment, incoherence) in the context of other psychotic symptoms?**
  - YES → Psychotic disorder — Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Are there persistent difficulties in the acquisition and use of language?
- **Q: Are there persistent difficulties in the acquisition and use of language?**
  - YES → Language Disorder — Language Disorder (differential Table 3.1.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there difficulties with speech sound production that reduce intelligibility?
- **Q: Are there difficulties with speech sound production that reduce intelligibility?**
  - YES → Speech Sound Disorder — Speech Sound Disorder (differential Table 3.1.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there disturbances in fluency and time patterning of speech inappropriate for age?
- **Q: Are there disturbances in fluency and time patterning of speech inappropriate for age?**
  - YES → Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (Stuttering) — Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (Stuttering) (differential Table 3.1.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there deficits in the social (pragmatic) use of verbal and nonverbal communication?
- **Q: Are there deficits in the social (pragmatic) use of verbal and nonverbal communication?**
  - YES → ASD or Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder — Autism Spectrum Disorder (differential Table 3.1.3); Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder (differential Table 3.1.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there inappropriate vocal outbursts in the context of otherwise normal speech?

  - YES → Tic Disorder — Tic Disorder (differential Table 3.1.6)
  - NO → Consider residual category or no mental disorder

### Tree 2.4 — Distractibility

_Presenting: Distractibility / difficulty sustaining attention · Group: Neurodevelopmental presentations_

Reduced ability to sustain attention is nonspecific and occurs across many disorders. Rule out substances and medical causes, then distinguish attentional problems that are episodic and mood-linked (mania, depression, anxiety, psychosis) from the persistent, cross-situational, early-onset pattern of ADHD.

- **Q: Is the distractibility due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication)?**
  - YES → Substance-induced — Substance Intoxication/Withdrawal or Substance-Induced Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (including delirium)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (including delirium)?**
  - YES → Medical etiology / Delirium — Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1); Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only during periods of elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy?
- **Q: Does it occur only during periods of elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy?**
  - YES → Manic/Hypomanic Episode — Bipolar I/II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only during a Major Depressive Episode?
- **Q: Does it occur only during a Major Depressive Episode?**
  - YES → Major Depressive Episode — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only in the context of prominent anxiety or worry?
- **Q: Does it occur only in the context of prominent anxiety or worry?**
  - YES → An anxiety disorder — Generalized Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.7) (see Tree 2.13)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only in the context of psychotic symptoms?
- **Q: Does it occur only in the context of psychotic symptoms?**
  - YES → A psychotic disorder — Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Is there a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, onset before age 12, impairing across settings?
- **Q: Is there a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, onset before age 12, impairing across settings?**
  - YES → Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (differential Table 3.1.4)
  - NO → next question: Are there persistent deficits in social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviors (ASD)?
- **Q: Are there persistent deficits in social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviors (ASD)?**
  - YES → Autism Spectrum Disorder — Autism Spectrum Disorder (differential Table 3.1.3)
  - NO → Distractibility not attributable to a specific mental disorder (or subthreshold) — consider context

### Tree 2.5 — Delusions

_Presenting: Delusions (fixed false beliefs) · Group: Schizophrenia & other psychotic presentations_

First confirm the belief is a true delusion and not a culturally or religiously sanctioned belief. Then apply the etiologic rule-outs (substance, medical) before differentiating among the primary psychotic and mood disorders. The mood/psychosis relationship — whether delusions are confined to mood episodes, and the proportion of the illness occupied by mood episodes — drives the schizophrenia vs. schizoaffective vs. mood-disorder-with-psychotic-features distinctions.

- **Q: Is the belief a manifestation of a culturally or religiously sanctioned belief system?**
  - YES → Not a delusion — no diagnosis on this basis
  - NO → next question: Are the delusions due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication)?
- **Q: Are the delusions due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?
- **Q: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Do the delusions occur exclusively during episodes of elevated/expansive/irritable mood?
- **Q: Do the delusions occur exclusively during episodes of elevated/expansive/irritable mood?**
  - YES → Bipolar Disorder, With Psychotic Features — Bipolar I/II Disorder, With Psychotic Features (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Do the delusions occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?
- **Q: Do the delusions occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?**
  - YES → Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features — Major Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Have the delusions (with other active-phase symptoms) persisted for 6 months or more?
- **Q: Have the delusions (with other active-phase symptoms) persisted for 6 months or more?**
  - _Duration and the mood relationship separate schizophrenia, schizophreniform, schizoaffective, brief psychotic, and delusional disorder._
  - YES → next question: Are the delusions accompanied by other characteristic psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, or negative symptoms)?
  - NO → next question: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 month but less than 6 months?
- **Q: Are the delusions accompanied by other characteristic psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, or negative symptoms)?**
  - YES → next question: Is there a history of Major Depressive or Manic Episodes overlapping the active psychotic period?
  - NO → Delusional Disorder — Delusional Disorder (differential Table 3.2.3)
- **Q: Is there a history of Major Depressive or Manic Episodes overlapping the active psychotic period?**
  - YES → next question: Have mood episodes been present for the MAJORITY of the total active/residual illness, AND were there ≥2 weeks of delusions/hallucinations without a mood episode?
  - NO → Schizophrenia — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1)
- **Q: Have mood episodes been present for the MAJORITY of the total active/residual illness, AND were there ≥2 weeks of delusions/hallucinations without a mood episode?**
  - _Schizoaffective requires a concurrent mood episode for the majority of the illness plus ≥2 weeks of psychosis without prominent mood symptoms. If mood episodes occupy only a minority, favor Schizophrenia; if psychosis is confined to mood episodes, favor a mood disorder with psychotic features._
  - YES → Schizoaffective Disorder — Schizoaffective Disorder (differential Table 3.2.2)
  - NO → Schizophrenia (mood episodes a minority of illness) — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1)
- **Q: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 month but less than 6 months?**
  - YES → Schizophreniform Disorder — Schizophreniform Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1)
  - NO → next question: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 day but less than 1 month with return to premorbid functioning?
- **Q: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 day but less than 1 month with return to premorbid functioning?**
  - YES → Brief Psychotic Disorder — Brief Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.4)
  - NO → next question: Are the delusion-like beliefs subthreshold or better explained by another disorder (e.g., OCD, body dysmorphic disorder, delusional-level)?
- **Q: Are the delusion-like beliefs subthreshold or better explained by another disorder (e.g., OCD, body dysmorphic disorder, delusional-level)?**
  - YES → Consider OCD/BDD with absent insight or another disorder — Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (differential Table 3.6.1); Body Dysmorphic Disorder (differential Table 3.6.2)
  - NO → Other Specified / Unspecified Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorder

### Tree 2.6 — Hallucinations

_Presenting: Hallucinations (perceptions without external stimulus) · Group: Schizophrenia & other psychotic presentations_

Distinguish true hallucinations from normal experiences (hypnagogic/hypnopompic phenomena, intense imagery) and illusions. The branching parallels the delusions tree: rule out substance and medical etiologies, then use the mood/psychosis relationship to differentiate the primary disorders. Hallucinations in clear consciousness confined to one modality, or occurring only at sleep-wake transitions, have distinct implications.

- **Q: Do the hallucinations occur only while falling asleep or waking (hypnagogic/hypnopompic)?**
  - YES → Normal sleep-related phenomena (consider narcolepsy if with cataplexy/other features) — Narcolepsy (if other features present) (see Tree 2.20)
  - NO → next question: Are the hallucinations due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication or withdrawal)?
- **Q: Are the hallucinations due to the physiological effects of a substance (including medication or withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder (or intoxication/withdrawal) — Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (including delirium)?
- **Q: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (including delirium)?**
  - YES → Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition / Delirium — Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29); Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur exclusively during episodes of elevated/expansive/irritable mood?
- **Q: Do they occur exclusively during episodes of elevated/expansive/irritable mood?**
  - YES → Bipolar Disorder, With Psychotic Features — Bipolar I/II Disorder, With Psychotic Features (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?
- **Q: Do they occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?**
  - YES → Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features — Major Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Are the hallucinations part of a re-experiencing response to a traumatic event (flashbacks)?
- **Q: Are the hallucinations part of a re-experiencing response to a traumatic event (flashbacks)?**
  - YES → PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Have psychotic symptoms persisted for 6 months or more?
- **Q: Have psychotic symptoms persisted for 6 months or more?**
  - YES → next question: Are they accompanied by other characteristic psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia?
  - NO → next question: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 month but less than 6 months?
- **Q: Are they accompanied by other characteristic psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia?**
  - YES → next question: Is there a history of Major Depressive or Manic Episodes overlapping the active psychotic period?
  - NO → Delusional Disorder (if delusions predominate) or reconsider — Delusional Disorder (differential Table 3.2.3)
- **Q: Is there a history of Major Depressive or Manic Episodes overlapping the active psychotic period?**
  - YES → next question: Have mood episodes been present for the majority of the illness, with ≥2 weeks of psychosis without a mood episode?
  - NO → Schizophrenia — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1)
- **Q: Have mood episodes been present for the majority of the illness, with ≥2 weeks of psychosis without a mood episode?**
  - YES → Schizoaffective Disorder — Schizoaffective Disorder (differential Table 3.2.2)
  - NO → Schizophrenia (mood a minority of illness) — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1)
- **Q: Has the disturbance lasted at least 1 month but less than 6 months?**
  - YES → Schizophreniform Disorder — Schizophreniform Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1)
  - NO → next question: Has it lasted at least 1 day but less than 1 month with full recovery?
- **Q: Has it lasted at least 1 day but less than 1 month with full recovery?**
  - YES → Brief Psychotic Disorder — Brief Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.4)
  - NO → Other Specified / Unspecified Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorder

### Tree 2.7 — Catatonic Symptoms

_Presenting: Catatonic symptoms (stupor, mutism, posturing, rigidity, echophenomena, excitement) · Group: Schizophrenia & other psychotic presentations_

Catatonia is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. It occurs with medical conditions, psychotic disorders, and mood disorders, and it can be life-threatening (malignant catatonia). Identify the underlying context; DSM-5 codes catatonia as a specifier of another mental disorder, catatonia due to another medical condition, or unspecified catatonia.

- **Q: Are the catatonic symptoms due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Catatonic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Catatonic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Are they due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., neuroleptic malignant syndrome, intoxication)?
- **Q: Are they due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., neuroleptic malignant syndrome, intoxication)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced (consider NMS as a medical emergency) — Medication-Induced Movement Disorder / Substance-Induced state (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur in the context of a psychotic disorder (schizophrenia, schizophreniform, schizoaffective, brief psychotic)?
- **Q: Do they occur in the context of a psychotic disorder (schizophrenia, schizophreniform, schizoaffective, brief psychotic)?**
  - YES → Catatonia associated with a psychotic disorder — Schizophrenia (with catatonia) (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur in the context of a Manic or Major Depressive Episode?
- **Q: Do they occur in the context of a Manic or Major Depressive Episode?**
  - YES → Catatonia associated with a mood disorder — Bipolar or Depressive Disorder (with catatonia) (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Do they occur with the social-communication deficits and restricted/repetitive behaviors of autism spectrum disorder?
- **Q: Do they occur with the social-communication deficits and restricted/repetitive behaviors of autism spectrum disorder?**
  - YES → Catatonia associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder — Autism Spectrum Disorder (with catatonia) (differential Table 3.1.3)
  - NO → next question: Is the clinical picture a catatonia syndrome where the underlying disorder is not yet established?
- **Q: Is the clinical picture a catatonia syndrome where the underlying disorder is not yet established?**
  - YES → Unspecified Catatonia — Unspecified Catatonia (differential Table 3.2.5)
  - NO → Reconsider whether criteria for catatonia are met

### Tree 2.8 — Elevated or Expansive Mood

_Presenting: Elevated, expansive mood with increased energy/activity · Group: Bipolar presentations_

Establish whether the mood episode is a full Manic Episode (≥1 week or hospitalization, marked impairment/psychosis) or a Hypomanic Episode (≥4 days, observable but not markedly impairing, no psychosis). Manic and hypomanic episodes are the building blocks for the bipolar disorders. Always rule out substance/medication and medical causes first.

- **Q: Is the mood disturbance due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (including antidepressant-triggered switch persisting beyond physiological effect)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar and Related Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar and Related Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Bipolar and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Bipolar and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does the episode last ≥1 week (or require hospitalization) with marked impairment or psychotic features?
- **Q: Does the episode last ≥1 week (or require hospitalization) with marked impairment or psychotic features?**
  - _This defines a Manic Episode._
  - YES → next question: Manic Episode confirmed — is there any additional context that changes coding (e.g., psychotic features, mixed features)?
  - NO → next question: Does the episode last ≥4 days with an observable, uncharacteristic change but without marked impairment or psychosis (Hypomanic Episode)?
- **Q: Manic Episode confirmed — is there any additional context that changes coding (e.g., psychotic features, mixed features)?**
  - YES → Bipolar I Disorder (specify features) — Bipolar I Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1)
  - NO → Bipolar I Disorder — Bipolar I Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1)
- **Q: Does the episode last ≥4 days with an observable, uncharacteristic change but without marked impairment or psychosis (Hypomanic Episode)?**
  - YES → next question: Is there also a history of at least one Major Depressive Episode (and never a full Manic Episode)?
  - NO → next question: Over ≥2 years, are there numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria?
- **Q: Is there also a history of at least one Major Depressive Episode (and never a full Manic Episode)?**
  - YES → Bipolar II Disorder — Bipolar II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.2)
  - NO → Other Specified Bipolar and Related Disorder (or reassess for prior mania/MDE) — Other Specified Bipolar and Related Disorder (differential Table 3.3.2)
- **Q: Over ≥2 years, are there numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria?**
  - YES → Cyclothymic Disorder — Cyclothymic Disorder (differential Table 3.3.3)
  - NO → next question: Do elevated-mood symptoms occur but fall below threshold and cause clinically significant distress/impairment?
- **Q: Do elevated-mood symptoms occur but fall below threshold and cause clinically significant distress/impairment?**
  - YES → Other Specified / Unspecified Bipolar and Related Disorder — Other Specified / Unspecified Bipolar and Related Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1)
  - NO → Elevated mood not indicative of a bipolar disorder (e.g., normal happiness, temperament)

### Tree 2.9 — Irritable Mood

_Presenting: Irritable mood · Group: Bipolar presentations_

Irritability is highly nonspecific and appears in mania, depression, anxiety, disruptive disorders, DMDD, personality disorders, PTSD, and as a normal reaction. Rule out substance/medical causes, then determine whether irritability marks a manic or depressive episode before considering the developmental and disruptive disorders.

- **Q: Is the irritability due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Mental Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur during a period of elevated energy/activity meeting manic or hypomanic criteria?
- **Q: Does it occur during a period of elevated energy/activity meeting manic or hypomanic criteria?**
  - YES → Manic/Hypomanic Episode — Bipolar I/II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur as part of a Major Depressive Episode or persistent depressive symptoms?
- **Q: Does it occur as part of a Major Depressive Episode or persistent depressive symptoms?**
  - YES → A depressive disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10); Persistent Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.2)
  - NO → next question: In a child ≤18 (onset before 10): severe recurrent temper outbursts with persistently irritable/angry mood between them, ≥12 months?
- **Q: In a child ≤18 (onset before 10): severe recurrent temper outbursts with persistently irritable/angry mood between them, ≥12 months?**
  - YES → Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder — Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (differential Table 3.4.4)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur with excessive anxiety/worry (e.g., generalized anxiety) or after trauma (PTSD)?
- **Q: Does it occur with excessive anxiety/worry (e.g., generalized anxiety) or after trauma (PTSD)?**
  - YES → Anxiety or trauma-related disorder — Generalized Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.7) (see Tree 2.13); Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Is it part of a pattern of argumentative/defiant behavior (ODD) or recurrent aggressive outbursts (intermittent explosive disorder)?
- **Q: Is it part of a pattern of argumentative/defiant behavior (ODD) or recurrent aggressive outbursts (intermittent explosive disorder)?**
  - YES → A disruptive/impulse-control disorder — Oppositional Defiant Disorder (differential Table 3.14.1); Intermittent Explosive Disorder (differential Table 3.14.2) (see Tree 2.23)
  - NO → next question: Is it a maladaptive response to a psychosocial stressor?
- **Q: Is it a maladaptive response to a psychosocial stressor?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → Irritability not indicative of a mental disorder (temperament, situational)

### Tree 2.10 — Depressed Mood

_Presenting: Depressed mood · Group: Depressive presentations_

One of the most common presentations. After ruling out substance and medical etiologies, determine whether the depressed mood constitutes a Major Depressive Episode, then whether there is any history of mania/hypomania (which reclassifies the case as bipolar), and how psychotic symptoms relate to the mood episodes. Subthreshold, persistent, and stressor-related presentations are captured at the end.

- **Q: Is the depressed mood due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is there ≥2 weeks of depressed mood and/or anhedonia with ≥5 total depressive symptoms (a Major Depressive Episode)?
- **Q: Is there ≥2 weeks of depressed mood and/or anhedonia with ≥5 total depressive symptoms (a Major Depressive Episode)?**
  - YES → next question: Is there any lifetime history of a Manic or Hypomanic Episode?
  - NO → next question: Is there depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (≥1 year in children/adolescents)?
- **Q: Is there any lifetime history of a Manic or Hypomanic Episode?**
  - _A single lifetime manic/hypomanic episode reclassifies the case as bipolar._
  - YES → A bipolar disorder — Bipolar I Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8); Bipolar II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.2)
  - NO → next question: Is there a history of delusions or hallucinations?
- **Q: Is there a history of delusions or hallucinations?**
  - YES → next question: Do the psychotic symptoms occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?
  - NO → Major Depressive Disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1)
- **Q: Do the psychotic symptoms occur exclusively during Major Depressive Episodes?**
  - YES → Major Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features — Major Depressive Disorder, With Psychotic Features (differential Table 3.4.1)
  - NO → A Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder is present — Schizoaffective Disorder (differential Table 3.2.2) (see Tree 2.5)
- **Q: Is there depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (≥1 year in children/adolescents)?**
  - YES → Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) — Persistent Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.2)
  - NO → next question: Do mood symptoms occur in the final premenstrual week and remit after menses, across most cycles?
- **Q: Do mood symptoms occur in the final premenstrual week and remit after menses, across most cycles?**
  - YES → Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder — Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (differential Table 3.4.3)
  - NO → next question: Is the depressed mood a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor?
- **Q: Is the depressed mood a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder, With Depressed Mood — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → next question: Are clinically significant depressive symptoms present but below threshold for the above?
- **Q: Are clinically significant depressive symptoms present but below threshold for the above?**
  - YES → Other Specified / Unspecified Depressive Disorder — Other Specified / Unspecified Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1)
  - NO → Sadness not indicative of a mental disorder (e.g., ordinary sadness, uncomplicated grief)

### Tree 2.11 — Suicidal Ideation or Behavior

_Presenting: Suicidal ideation or behavior · Group: Depressive presentations_

Suicidal ideation and behavior cut across nearly every diagnostic grouping and demand immediate risk assessment independent of diagnosis. Diagnostically, identify the disorder(s) in which the ideation is occurring; treatment and safety planning take priority over nosology. This tree points to the disorders most commonly associated with suicidality.

- **Q: Is the ideation/behavior occurring in the context of a substance intoxication/withdrawal state or medication effect?**
  - YES → Address substance state; a substance-induced mood disorder may be present — Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it occurring during a Major Depressive Episode?
- **Q: Is it occurring during a Major Depressive Episode?**
  - YES → Major Depressive Episode context — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10); Bipolar Disorder, depressed (differential Table 3.3.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it occurring in the context of a psychotic disorder (e.g., command hallucinations, delusions)?
- **Q: Is it occurring in the context of a psychotic disorder (e.g., command hallucinations, delusions)?**
  - YES → Psychotic disorder context — Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Is it occurring after a traumatic event with PTSD features?
- **Q: Is it occurring after a traumatic event with PTSD features?**
  - YES → PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder context — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Is the self-harm/ideation part of a pattern of affective instability, identity disturbance, and impulsivity?
- **Q: Is the self-harm/ideation part of a pattern of affective instability, identity disturbance, and impulsivity?**
  - YES → Borderline Personality Disorder (also see self-injury tree) — Borderline Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.5) (see Tree 2.25)
  - NO → next question: Is it a maladaptive response to a recent psychosocial stressor without another disorder?
- **Q: Is it a maladaptive response to a recent psychosocial stressor without another disorder?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → Suicidal ideation/behavior not explained by a specific disorder — continue risk assessment and monitor

### Tree 2.12 — Psychomotor Retardation

_Presenting: Psychomotor retardation (slowed movement, speech, thought) · Group: Depressive presentations_

Slowing of movement and thought can reflect depression, catatonia, parkinsonism (including medication-induced), hypoactive delirium, neurocognitive disorders, and negative symptoms of psychosis. Rule out substance/medication (e.g., neuroleptics) and medical causes before attributing it to a primary mood or psychotic disorder.

- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., sedatives, antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced state (e.g., medication-induced parkinsonism) — Medication-Induced Movement Disorder / Substance-Induced state (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., Parkinson's disease, hypothyroidism, hypoactive delirium)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., Parkinson's disease, hypothyroidism, hypoactive delirium)?**
  - YES → Medical etiology (including delirium) — Delirium / Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (differential Table 3.16.1) (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does it meet criteria for catatonia (with stupor, mutism, posturing, negativism)?
- **Q: Does it meet criteria for catatonia (with stupor, mutism, posturing, negativism)?**
  - YES → Catatonia — Catatonia (differential Table 3.2.5) (see Tree 2.7)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur as part of a Major Depressive or Manic Episode (mixed/with psychomotor changes)?
- **Q: Does it occur as part of a Major Depressive or Manic Episode (mixed/with psychomotor changes)?**
  - YES → A mood disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur as a negative symptom of a psychotic disorder?
- **Q: Does it occur as a negative symptom of a psychotic disorder?**
  - YES → Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder — Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → Consider neurocognitive disorder or reassess — not attributable to a specific mood/psychotic disorder

### Tree 2.13 — Anxiety

_Presenting: Anxiety (fear, worry, apprehension) · Group: Anxiety presentations_

After ruling out substance and medical causes, the anxiety disorders are differentiated by the FOCUS of the fear/anxiety: separation from attachment figures, specific objects/situations, social scrutiny, unexpected panic and its consequences, or pervasive worry across domains. Obsessive-compulsive and trauma-related disorders are considered when the anxiety is tied to obsessions/compulsions or to a traumatic event.

- **Q: Is the anxiety due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., caffeine, stimulants, withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., hyperthyroidism, arrhythmia)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., hyperthyroidism, arrhythmia)?**
  - YES → Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is the anxiety focused on separation from attachment figures?
- **Q: Is the anxiety focused on separation from attachment figures?**
  - YES → Separation Anxiety Disorder — Separation Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it cued by a specific object or situation (e.g., heights, animals, injections)?
- **Q: Is it cued by a specific object or situation (e.g., heights, animals, injections)?**
  - YES → Specific Phobia — Specific Phobia (differential Table 3.5.3)
  - NO → next question: Is it focused on social situations involving possible scrutiny by others?
- **Q: Is it focused on social situations involving possible scrutiny by others?**
  - YES → Social Anxiety Disorder — Social Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.4)
  - NO → next question: Are there recurrent unexpected panic attacks with persistent worry about further attacks or their consequences?
- **Q: Are there recurrent unexpected panic attacks with persistent worry about further attacks or their consequences?**
  - YES → Panic Disorder — Panic Disorder (differential Table 3.5.5) (see Tree 2.14)
  - NO → next question: Is it focused on situations where escape may be difficult or help unavailable (e.g., public transport, open/enclosed spaces, crowds)?
- **Q: Is it focused on situations where escape may be difficult or help unavailable (e.g., public transport, open/enclosed spaces, crowds)?**
  - YES → Agoraphobia — Agoraphobia (differential Table 3.5.6)
  - NO → next question: Is it driven by obsessions and/or compulsions?
- **Q: Is it driven by obsessions and/or compulsions?**
  - YES → Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder — Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (differential Table 3.6.1)
  - NO → next question: Does it follow exposure to a traumatic event with re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal?
- **Q: Does it follow exposure to a traumatic event with re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal?**
  - YES → PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Is there excessive worry about multiple events/activities, more days than not for ≥6 months, hard to control?
- **Q: Is there excessive worry about multiple events/activities, more days than not for ≥6 months, hard to control?**
  - YES → Generalized Anxiety Disorder — Generalized Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.7)
  - NO → next question: Is the anxiety a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor?
- **Q: Is the anxiety a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder, With Anxiety — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → next question: Are clinically significant anxiety symptoms present but below threshold for a specific disorder?
- **Q: Are clinically significant anxiety symptoms present but below threshold for a specific disorder?**
  - YES → Other Specified / Unspecified Anxiety Disorder — Other Specified / Unspecified Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.7)
  - NO → Anxiety within normal limits — not a mental disorder

### Tree 2.14 — Panic Attacks

_Presenting: Panic attacks · Group: Anxiety presentations_

A panic attack is a symptom, not a disorder, and can occur in any anxiety disorder and many other conditions. The key distinction is between UNEXPECTED attacks (occurring without an obvious cue) and EXPECTED attacks (cued by a feared object/situation). Recurrent unexpected attacks plus anticipatory worry define panic disorder; expected attacks point to the disorder tied to their trigger.

- **Q: Are the attacks due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., stimulant, caffeine, withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., arrhythmia, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma)?
- **Q: Are they due to the physiological effects of a general medical condition (e.g., arrhythmia, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma)?**
  - YES → Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Are the attacks cued exclusively by a specific phobic object/situation?
- **Q: Are the attacks cued exclusively by a specific phobic object/situation?**
  - YES → Specific Phobia — Specific Phobia (differential Table 3.5.3)
  - NO → next question: Are they cued exclusively by social scrutiny?
- **Q: Are they cued exclusively by social scrutiny?**
  - YES → Social Anxiety Disorder — Social Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.4)
  - NO → next question: Are they cued exclusively by agoraphobic situations?
- **Q: Are they cued exclusively by agoraphobic situations?**
  - YES → Agoraphobia — Agoraphobia (differential Table 3.5.6)
  - NO → next question: Are they cued exclusively by reminders of a traumatic event?
- **Q: Are they cued exclusively by reminders of a traumatic event?**
  - YES → PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Are at least some attacks unexpected, with ≥1 month of worry about further attacks or maladaptive change in behavior?
- **Q: Are at least some attacks unexpected, with ≥1 month of worry about further attacks or maladaptive change in behavior?**
  - YES → Panic Disorder — Panic Disorder (differential Table 3.5.5)
  - NO → Panic attacks present as a specifier of another disorder, or subthreshold — reassess the primary condition

### Tree 2.15 — Avoidance Behavior

_Presenting: Avoidance behavior · Group: Anxiety presentations_

Avoidance is a final common pathway of many disorders; the diagnostic question is WHAT is being avoided and WHY. Identify the motivation for avoidance — feared objects, social scrutiny, panic-prone situations, trauma reminders, obsessional triggers, appearance concerns, or separation — to reach the underlying disorder.

- **Q: Is the avoidance due to a substance/medication or general medical condition affecting the CNS?**
  - YES → Substance/medical etiology — Substance/Medication-Induced or Medical Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is avoidance driven by fear of specific objects/situations?
- **Q: Is avoidance driven by fear of specific objects/situations?**
  - YES → Specific Phobia — Specific Phobia (differential Table 3.5.3)
  - NO → next question: Is it driven by fear of social scrutiny/negative evaluation?
- **Q: Is it driven by fear of social scrutiny/negative evaluation?**
  - YES → Social Anxiety Disorder — Social Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.4)
  - NO → next question: Is it driven by fear of panic-like symptoms in situations where escape is hard (agoraphobia)?
- **Q: Is it driven by fear of panic-like symptoms in situations where escape is hard (agoraphobia)?**
  - YES → Agoraphobia — Agoraphobia (differential Table 3.5.6)
  - NO → next question: Is it avoidance of reminders of a traumatic event?
- **Q: Is it avoidance of reminders of a traumatic event?**
  - YES → PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Is it avoidance triggered by obsessions (to prevent feared outcomes)?
- **Q: Is it avoidance triggered by obsessions (to prevent feared outcomes)?**
  - YES → Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder — Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (differential Table 3.6.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it avoidance driven by perceived appearance flaws?
- **Q: Is it avoidance driven by perceived appearance flaws?**
  - YES → Body Dysmorphic Disorder — Body Dysmorphic Disorder (differential Table 3.6.2)
  - NO → next question: Is it avoidance of separation from attachment figures?
- **Q: Is it avoidance of separation from attachment figures?**
  - YES → Separation Anxiety Disorder — Separation Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.5.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it a pervasive pattern of social inhibition and avoidance due to feelings of inadequacy and fear of criticism (personality-level)?
- **Q: Is it a pervasive pattern of social inhibition and avoidance due to feelings of inadequacy and fear of criticism (personality-level)?**
  - YES → Avoidant Personality Disorder — Avoidant Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.8)
  - NO → Avoidance not explained by a specific disorder — reassess motivation and context

### Tree 2.16 — Trauma or Psychosocial Stressors in the Etiology

_Presenting: Symptoms arising after a trauma or psychosocial stressor · Group: Trauma- & stressor-related presentations_

When symptoms follow an identifiable stressor, the differential turns on the nature of the stressor (traumatic vs. ordinary), the symptom profile, and timing/duration. Traumatic stressors with characteristic re-experiencing, avoidance, cognition/mood changes, and hyperarousal point to PTSD or acute stress disorder; a broader maladaptive response to a non-traumatic stressor suggests adjustment disorder. Other disorders can also be precipitated by stress.

- **Q: Was the stressor an exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence (a traumatic stressor)?**
  - YES → next question: Are there characteristic symptoms — intrusion/re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, and arousal?
  - NO → next question: In a child with pathogenic care, is there a pattern of inhibited/emotionally withdrawn behavior (or indiscriminate social behavior)?
- **Q: Are there characteristic symptoms — intrusion/re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, and arousal?**
  - YES → next question: Has the full symptom picture persisted for more than 1 month?
  - NO → next question: Are there prominent dissociative symptoms (amnesia, depersonalization/derealization) related to the trauma?
- **Q: Has the full symptom picture persisted for more than 1 month?**
  - YES → Posttraumatic Stress Disorder — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1)
  - NO → Acute Stress Disorder (3 days to 1 month) — Acute Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1)
- **Q: Are there prominent dissociative symptoms (amnesia, depersonalization/derealization) related to the trauma?**
  - YES → A dissociative disorder — Dissociative Amnesia (differential Table 3.8.1); Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder (differential Table 3.8.2)
  - NO → next question: In a child with pathogenic care, is there a pattern of inhibited/emotionally withdrawn behavior (or indiscriminate social behavior)?
- **Q: In a child with pathogenic care, is there a pattern of inhibited/emotionally withdrawn behavior (or indiscriminate social behavior)?**
  - YES → Reactive Attachment Disorder or Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder — Reactive Attachment Disorder / Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1)
  - NO → next question: Do full criteria for another disorder (e.g., major depressive, anxiety, psychotic) precipitated by the stressor apply?
- **Q: Do full criteria for another disorder (e.g., major depressive, anxiety, psychotic) precipitated by the stressor apply?**
  - YES → Diagnose the specific precipitated disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Are there clinically significant emotional/behavioral symptoms within 3 months of a stressor, in excess of expectation, not meeting another disorder?
- **Q: Are there clinically significant emotional/behavioral symptoms within 3 months of a stressor, in excess of expectation, not meeting another disorder?**
  - YES → Adjustment Disorder — Adjustment Disorder (differential Table 3.7.2)
  - NO → Normal stress reaction / not a mental disorder

### Tree 2.17 — Somatic Complaints or Illness/Appearance Anxiety

_Presenting: Somatic complaints or health/appearance anxiety · Group: Somatic symptom presentations_

First ensure a general medical condition has been appropriately evaluated. The somatic symptom and related disorders are differentiated by whether distressing somatic symptoms are present, whether the focus is on having/acquiring a serious illness versus the symptoms themselves, whether there is deceptive symptom production, and whether neurological symptoms are incompatible with recognized disease.

- **Q: Are the symptoms fully explained by a general medical condition (with a proportionate response)?**
  - YES → General medical condition (consider Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions if psychological factors adversely affect it) — Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions (differential Table 3.9.4)
  - NO → next question: Are symptoms intentionally produced/feigned for external incentives (malingering) or to assume the sick role (factitious)?
- **Q: Are symptoms intentionally produced/feigned for external incentives (malingering) or to assume the sick role (factitious)?**
  - YES → Malingering (external incentive) or Factitious Disorder (no external incentive) — Factitious Disorder (differential Table 3.9.5)
  - NO → next question: Are there neurological symptoms (weakness, movement, sensory, seizures) clinically incompatible with recognized disease?
- **Q: Are there neurological symptoms (weakness, movement, sensory, seizures) clinically incompatible with recognized disease?**
  - YES → Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder) — Conversion Disorder (differential Table 3.9.3)
  - NO → next question: Are there ≥1 distressing somatic symptoms with excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviors about them?
- **Q: Are there ≥1 distressing somatic symptoms with excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviors about them?**
  - YES → Somatic Symptom Disorder — Somatic Symptom Disorder (differential Table 3.9.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there preoccupation with having/acquiring a serious illness, with minimal or no somatic symptoms and high health anxiety?
- **Q: Is there preoccupation with having/acquiring a serious illness, with minimal or no somatic symptoms and high health anxiety?**
  - YES → Illness Anxiety Disorder — Illness Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.9.2)
  - NO → next question: Is there preoccupation with a perceived defect/flaw in physical appearance, with repetitive behaviors?
- **Q: Is there preoccupation with a perceived defect/flaw in physical appearance, with repetitive behaviors?**
  - YES → Body Dysmorphic Disorder — Body Dysmorphic Disorder (differential Table 3.6.2)
  - NO → next question: Are the somatic complaints better explained by a depressive, anxiety, or panic disorder?
- **Q: Are the somatic complaints better explained by a depressive, anxiety, or panic disorder?**
  - YES → Diagnose the underlying mood/anxiety disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10); Panic Disorder (differential Table 3.5.5) (see Tree 2.14)
  - NO → Other Specified / Unspecified Somatic Symptom and Related Disorder, or no disorder

### Tree 2.18 — Appetite Changes or Unusual Eating Behavior

_Presenting: Appetite change or unusual eating behavior · Group: Feeding & eating presentations_

Distinguish appetite/weight change occurring as part of another disorder (depression, mania, anxiety, psychosis) or a medical/substance cause from the primary feeding and eating disorders, which are differentiated by restriction and low weight (anorexia), binge-purge cycles (bulimia), binge eating without compensation (binge-eating disorder), avoidant/restrictive intake without body-image disturbance (ARFID), and eating of nonnutritive substances (pica) or regurgitation (rumination).

- **Q: Is the appetite/eating change due to a substance/medication or general medical condition?**
  - YES → Substance/medical etiology — Substance/Medication-Induced or Medical cause (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur exclusively as part of a mood, anxiety, or psychotic disorder?
- **Q: Does it occur exclusively as part of a mood, anxiety, or psychotic disorder?**
  - YES → Diagnose the underlying disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Is there restriction of intake leading to significantly low body weight, fear of weight gain, and body-image disturbance?
- **Q: Is there restriction of intake leading to significantly low body weight, fear of weight gain, and body-image disturbance?**
  - YES → Anorexia Nervosa — Anorexia Nervosa (differential Table 3.10.2)
  - NO → next question: Are there recurrent binges with compensatory behaviors (vomiting, laxatives, fasting, exercise), self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape/weight, at normal-ish weight?
- **Q: Are there recurrent binges with compensatory behaviors (vomiting, laxatives, fasting, exercise), self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape/weight, at normal-ish weight?**
  - YES → Bulimia Nervosa — Bulimia Nervosa (differential Table 3.10.3)
  - NO → next question: Are there recurrent binges with marked distress but WITHOUT regular compensatory behavior?
- **Q: Are there recurrent binges with marked distress but WITHOUT regular compensatory behavior?**
  - YES → Binge-Eating Disorder — Binge-Eating Disorder (differential Table 3.10.4)
  - NO → next question: Is there avoidance/restriction of food (based on sensory features or aversive consequences) with nutritional/psychosocial impact but no body-image disturbance?
- **Q: Is there avoidance/restriction of food (based on sensory features or aversive consequences) with nutritional/psychosocial impact but no body-image disturbance?**
  - YES → Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder — Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (differential Table 3.10.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there persistent eating of nonnutritive substances (pica) or repeated regurgitation (rumination)?
- **Q: Is there persistent eating of nonnutritive substances (pica) or repeated regurgitation (rumination)?**
  - YES → Pica or Rumination Disorder — Pica / Rumination Disorder (differential Table 3.10.1)
  - NO → Other Specified / Unspecified Feeding or Eating Disorder, or no disorder

### Tree 2.19 — Insomnia

_Presenting: Insomnia (difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep) · Group: Sleep-wake presentations_

Determine whether insomnia is due to a substance/medication, another sleep-wake disorder (e.g., a breathing-related or circadian disorder), or a coexisting mental disorder, or whether it is a persistent, independent insomnia disorder. DSM-5 allows insomnia disorder to be diagnosed alongside comorbid conditions when it is an independent focus of treatment.

- **Q: Is the insomnia due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (including caffeine, withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it explained by another sleep-wake disorder (e.g., breathing-related, circadian rhythm, restless legs)?
- **Q: Is it explained by another sleep-wake disorder (e.g., breathing-related, circadian rhythm, restless legs)?**
  - YES → The specific sleep-wake disorder — Breathing-Related / Circadian Rhythm / Restless Legs / other sleep disorder
  - NO → next question: Is it due to another medical condition (e.g., pain, nocturia)?
- **Q: Is it due to another medical condition (e.g., pain, nocturia)?**
  - YES → Insomnia related to another medical condition — Sleep disturbance due to another medical condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does the insomnia occur only during and is fully accounted for by another mental disorder (e.g., MDD, GAD)?
- **Q: Does the insomnia occur only during and is fully accounted for by another mental disorder (e.g., MDD, GAD)?**
  - YES → Sleep disturbance as a feature of the underlying disorder — Major Depressive Disorder / Generalized Anxiety Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Is there dissatisfaction with sleep quantity/quality ≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months with daytime impairment (independent focus)?
- **Q: Is there dissatisfaction with sleep quantity/quality ≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months with daytime impairment (independent focus)?**
  - YES → Insomnia Disorder — Insomnia Disorder (differential Table 3.11.1)
  - NO → Situational/transient sleep difficulty — not a disorder

### Tree 2.20 — Hypersomnolence

_Presenting: Hypersomnolence (excessive sleepiness) · Group: Sleep-wake presentations_

Excessive daytime sleepiness can arise from insufficient sleep, substances, other sleep disorders (notably narcolepsy and breathing-related sleep disorder), medical conditions, and mood disorders. Cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic hallucinations point specifically to narcolepsy.

- **Q: Is the hypersomnolence due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is there cataplexy and/or evidence of hypocretin deficiency / characteristic REM findings?
- **Q: Is there cataplexy and/or evidence of hypocretin deficiency / characteristic REM findings?**
  - YES → Narcolepsy — Narcolepsy
  - NO → next question: Is it explained by a breathing-related sleep disorder (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea) or another sleep disorder?
- **Q: Is it explained by a breathing-related sleep disorder (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea) or another sleep disorder?**
  - YES → Breathing-Related Sleep Disorder / other sleep disorder — Breathing-Related Sleep Disorder
  - NO → next question: Is it due to another medical condition?
- **Q: Is it due to another medical condition?**
  - YES → Hypersomnolence due to another medical condition — Sleep disturbance due to another medical condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur exclusively as part of another mental disorder (e.g., a Major Depressive Episode with hypersomnia)?
- **Q: Does it occur exclusively as part of another mental disorder (e.g., a Major Depressive Episode with hypersomnia)?**
  - YES → Feature of the underlying mood disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Is there self-reported excessive sleepiness despite ≥7 hours sleep, ≥3 times/week for ≥3 months, with impairment (independent focus)?
- **Q: Is there self-reported excessive sleepiness despite ≥7 hours sleep, ≥3 times/week for ≥3 months, with impairment (independent focus)?**
  - YES → Hypersomnolence Disorder — Hypersomnolence Disorder (differential Table 3.11.2)
  - NO → Sleepiness due to insufficient sleep or situational factors — not a disorder

### Tree 2.21 — Sexual Dysfunction in a Female

_Presenting: Sexual dysfunction in a female · Group: Sexual dysfunction presentations_

Sexual dysfunctions require clinically significant distress and typically ≥6 months' duration. Exclude substance/medication effects, medical conditions, severe relationship distress, and other mental disorders that better account for the problem before diagnosing a primary sexual dysfunction, which is then specified by the phase/nature of the difficulty.

- **Q: Is the dysfunction due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., SSRIs)?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction — Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction (differential Table 3.12.1) (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it fully explained by a general medical condition?
- **Q: Is it fully explained by a general medical condition?**
  - YES → Sexual dysfunction due to another medical condition (coded medically) — Sexual dysfunction attributable to a medical condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is it better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., major depressive disorder) or by severe relationship distress/other stressors?
- **Q: Is it better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., major depressive disorder) or by severe relationship distress/other stressors?**
  - YES → Diagnose the underlying disorder / note the stressor — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Is there reduced/absent sexual interest and arousal?
- **Q: Is there reduced/absent sexual interest and arousal?**
  - YES → Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder — Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder (differential Table 3.12.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there marked delay/absence/reduced intensity of orgasm?
- **Q: Is there marked delay/absence/reduced intensity of orgasm?**
  - YES → Female Orgasmic Disorder — Female Orgasmic Disorder (differential Table 3.12.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there marked pain, tension, or fear with vaginal penetration?
- **Q: Is there marked pain, tension, or fear with vaginal penetration?**
  - YES → Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder — Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder (differential Table 3.12.1)
  - NO → Other Specified / Unspecified Sexual Dysfunction, or no disorder (if not distressing)

### Tree 2.22 — Sexual Dysfunction in a Male

_Presenting: Sexual dysfunction in a male · Group: Sexual dysfunction presentations_

As with female sexual dysfunctions, require clinically significant distress and typically ≥6 months' duration, and exclude substance/medication effects, medical conditions, severe relationship distress, and other mental disorders before diagnosing a primary male sexual dysfunction, specified by desire, arousal/erectile, or ejaculatory phase.

- **Q: Is the dysfunction due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction — Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction (differential Table 3.12.1) (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it fully explained by a general medical condition (e.g., vascular, neurologic, endocrine)?
- **Q: Is it fully explained by a general medical condition (e.g., vascular, neurologic, endocrine)?**
  - YES → Sexual dysfunction due to another medical condition — Sexual dysfunction attributable to a medical condition (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is it better explained by another mental disorder or severe relationship distress?
- **Q: Is it better explained by another mental disorder or severe relationship distress?**
  - YES → Diagnose the underlying disorder / note the stressor — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → next question: Is there persistently deficient sexual/erotic thoughts and desire?
- **Q: Is there persistently deficient sexual/erotic thoughts and desire?**
  - YES → Male Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder — Male Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (differential Table 3.12.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there difficulty obtaining/maintaining an erection or reduced erectile rigidity?
- **Q: Is there difficulty obtaining/maintaining an erection or reduced erectile rigidity?**
  - YES → Erectile Disorder — Erectile Disorder (differential Table 3.12.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there ejaculation occurring sooner than desired (premature) or marked delay/absence (delayed)?
- **Q: Is there ejaculation occurring sooner than desired (premature) or marked delay/absence (delayed)?**

### Tree 2.23 — Aggressive Behavior

_Presenting: Aggressive behavior · Group: Disruptive, impulse-control & conduct presentations_

Aggression is nonspecific. Rule out substance intoxication/withdrawal and medical causes (including delirium and neurocognitive disorders), then determine whether aggression is a feature of a mood, psychotic, developmental, trauma, or personality disorder, or a discrete impulse-control problem (intermittent explosive disorder, conduct disorder). Some aggression is purposeful and not a disorder.

- **Q: Is the aggression due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (intoxication/withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance-related etiology — Substance Intoxication/Withdrawal or Substance-Induced Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to a general medical condition (including delirium, neurocognitive disorder, personality change)?
- **Q: Is it due to a general medical condition (including delirium, neurocognitive disorder, personality change)?**
  - YES → Medical etiology — Delirium / Neurocognitive Disorder / Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition (differential Table 3.16.1) (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only during manic or psychotic episodes?
- **Q: Does it occur only during manic or psychotic episodes?**
  - YES → A mood or psychotic disorder — Bipolar I Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8); Schizophrenia (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: In youth: severe recurrent temper outbursts with persistent irritability between them (DMDD)?
- **Q: In youth: severe recurrent temper outbursts with persistent irritability between them (DMDD)?**
  - YES → Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder — Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (differential Table 3.4.4)
  - NO → next question: Does it follow trauma with PTSD-associated irritability/aggression?
- **Q: Does it follow trauma with PTSD-associated irritability/aggression?**
  - YES → PTSD — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (differential Table 3.7.1) (see Tree 2.16)
  - NO → next question: Are there recurrent impulsive aggressive outbursts grossly out of proportion to provocation, not premeditated?
- **Q: Are there recurrent impulsive aggressive outbursts grossly out of proportion to provocation, not premeditated?**
  - YES → Intermittent Explosive Disorder — Intermittent Explosive Disorder (differential Table 3.14.2)
  - NO → next question: Is it part of a repetitive pattern of violating others' rights/norms (with a callous style)?
- **Q: Is it part of a repetitive pattern of violating others' rights/norms (with a callous style)?**
  - YES → Conduct Disorder (youth) or Antisocial Personality Disorder (adult) — Conduct Disorder (differential Table 3.14.3); Antisocial Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.4)
  - NO → next question: Is it part of affective instability and impulsivity of borderline personality disorder?
- **Q: Is it part of affective instability and impulsivity of borderline personality disorder?**
  - YES → Borderline Personality Disorder — Borderline Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.5)
  - NO → Purposeful/instrumental aggression or reaction not attributable to a mental disorder

### Tree 2.24 — Impulsivity or Impulse-Control Problems

_Presenting: Impulsivity or impulse-control problems · Group: Disruptive, impulse-control & conduct presentations_

Impulsivity appears across mania, ADHD, substance use, personality disorders, and the discrete impulse-control disorders (each defined by the specific impulsive behavior). Rule out substance/medical causes and episodic mood states, then match the behavior to its disorder (stealing → kleptomania, fire setting → pyromania, gambling → gambling disorder, hair pulling → trichotillomania, skin picking → excoriation).

- **Q: Is the impulsivity due to a substance/medication or general medical condition?**
  - YES → Substance/medical etiology — Substance-Induced or Medical cause (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Does it occur only during manic/hypomanic episodes?
- **Q: Does it occur only during manic/hypomanic episodes?**
  - YES → A bipolar disorder — Bipolar I/II Disorder (differential Table 3.3.1) (see Tree 2.8)
  - NO → next question: Is there a persistent, cross-situational pattern of impulsivity with inattention/hyperactivity from childhood (ADHD)?
- **Q: Is there a persistent, cross-situational pattern of impulsivity with inattention/hyperactivity from childhood (ADHD)?**
  - YES → Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (differential Table 3.1.4)
  - NO → next question: Is the specific behavior recurrent stealing of unneeded objects?
- **Q: Is the specific behavior recurrent stealing of unneeded objects?**
  - YES → Kleptomania — Kleptomania
  - NO → next question: Is it deliberate fire setting with arousal/fascination?
- **Q: Is it deliberate fire setting with arousal/fascination?**
  - YES → Pyromania — Pyromania
  - NO → next question: Is it persistent, recurrent problematic gambling?
- **Q: Is it persistent, recurrent problematic gambling?**
  - YES → Gambling Disorder — Gambling Disorder (differential Table 3.15.2)
  - NO → next question: Is it recurrent hair pulling or skin picking with attempts to stop?
- **Q: Is it recurrent hair pulling or skin picking with attempts to stop?**
  - YES → Trichotillomania or Excoriation Disorder — Trichotillomania (differential Table 3.6.4); Excoriation (Skin-Picking) Disorder (differential Table 3.6.5)
  - NO → next question: Is impulsivity part of a pervasive personality pattern (borderline, antisocial)?
- **Q: Is impulsivity part of a pervasive personality pattern (borderline, antisocial)?**
  - YES → A personality disorder — Borderline Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.5); Antisocial Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.4)
  - NO → Impulsivity not attributable to a specific disorder — reassess context

### Tree 2.25 — Self-Injury or Self-Mutilation

_Presenting: Self-injury or self-mutilation · Group: Disruptive, impulse-control & conduct presentations_

Non-suicidal self-injury must be distinguished from suicidal behavior (see the suicidal ideation tree) and understood in its diagnostic context. Self-injury occurs with borderline personality disorder, in response to command hallucinations or delusions, in the context of stereotypies (intellectual disability, autism), and as a stress-related behavior. Always assess suicide risk in parallel.

- **Q: Is the self-injury intended to end one's life (suicidal)?**
  - YES → Assess as suicidal behavior — See Suicidal Ideation or Behavior tree (see Tree 2.11)
  - NO → next question: Is it driven by psychotic symptoms (command hallucinations, delusions)?
- **Q: Is it driven by psychotic symptoms (command hallucinations, delusions)?**
  - YES → A psychotic disorder — Schizophrenia Spectrum / Other Psychotic Disorder (differential Table 3.2.1) (see Tree 2.5)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to a substance/medication or general medical condition?
- **Q: Is it due to a substance/medication or general medical condition?**
  - YES → Substance/medical etiology — Substance-Induced or Medical cause (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is it repetitive, non-functional stereotyped self-injury with intellectual disability or autism?
- **Q: Is it repetitive, non-functional stereotyped self-injury with intellectual disability or autism?**
  - YES → Stereotypic Movement Disorder, With Self-Injurious Behavior — Stereotypic Movement Disorder
  - NO → next question: Does it occur in a pattern of affective instability, identity disturbance, and impulsivity?
- **Q: Does it occur in a pattern of affective instability, identity disturbance, and impulsivity?**
  - YES → Borderline Personality Disorder — Borderline Personality Disorder (differential Table 3.17.5)
  - NO → next question: Is it recurrent intentional self-injury to relieve negative feelings, without suicidal intent, and not better explained above?
- **Q: Is it recurrent intentional self-injury to relieve negative feelings, without suicidal intent, and not better explained above?**
  - YES → Non-Suicidal Self-Injury (condition for further study / Other Specified) — Non-Suicidal Self-Injury
  - NO → Reassess motivation and context

### Tree 2.26 — Excessive Substance Use

_Presenting: Excessive substance use · Group: Substance-related presentations_

Two questions run in parallel: is there a substance use disorder (a maladaptive pattern with impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological features), and are there substance-induced mental disorders (intoxication, withdrawal, or a substance-induced disorder such as psychotic, mood, or anxiety disorder)? This tree also anchors the substance-etiology decisions used throughout the other trees.

- **Q: Is there a problematic pattern of use with ≥2 of: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, tolerance/withdrawal, within 12 months?**
  - YES → Substance Use Disorder (specify substance and severity) — Substance Use Disorder (differential Table 3.15.1)
  - NO → next question: Are there reversible substance-specific symptoms from recent ingestion (intoxication)?
- **Q: Are there reversible substance-specific symptoms from recent ingestion (intoxication)?**
  - YES → Substance Intoxication — Substance Intoxication
  - NO → next question: Are there substance-specific symptoms from cessation/reduction after heavy prolonged use (withdrawal)?
- **Q: Are there substance-specific symptoms from cessation/reduction after heavy prolonged use (withdrawal)?**
  - YES → Substance Withdrawal — Substance Withdrawal
  - NO → next question: Are there prominent psychiatric symptoms (psychosis, mood, anxiety, sleep, sexual, cognitive) attributable to the substance and exceeding those expected of intoxication/withdrawal?
- **Q: Are there prominent psychiatric symptoms (psychosis, mood, anxiety, sleep, sexual, cognitive) attributable to the substance and exceeding those expected of intoxication/withdrawal?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Mental Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic/Depressive/Bipolar/Anxiety/Sleep/Sexual/Neurocognitive Disorder
  - NO → next question: Is the excessive use non-substance (behavioral), specifically persistent problematic gambling?
- **Q: Is the excessive use non-substance (behavioral), specifically persistent problematic gambling?**
  - YES → Gambling Disorder — Gambling Disorder (differential Table 3.15.2)
  - NO → Use present but not meeting disorder criteria — monitor / hazardous use (may warrant a Z code)

### Tree 2.27 — Memory Loss

_Presenting: Memory loss · Group: Neurocognitive presentations_

Distinguish amnesia due to a medical condition (including delirium and neurocognitive disorders) or a substance from dissociative amnesia (psychogenic, often for autobiographical/traumatic information, without a neurological basis) and from normal age-related forgetfulness. The presence/absence of other cognitive deficits, level of consciousness, and the nature of the forgotten material guide the differential.

- **Q: Is the memory loss accompanied by a fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness (delirium)?**
  - YES → Delirium — Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., alcohol blackout, benzodiazepines)?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication (e.g., alcohol blackout, benzodiazepines)?**
  - YES → Substance-Induced (e.g., substance-induced neurocognitive disorder) — Substance/Medication-Induced Neurocognitive Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to another medical condition, and accompanied by decline in memory (± other cognitive domains)?
- **Q: Is it due to another medical condition, and accompanied by decline in memory (± other cognitive domains)?**
  - YES → Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2) (see Tree 2.29)
  - NO → next question: Is there an inability to recall important autobiographical information (often stressful/traumatic), too extensive for ordinary forgetting, without a neurological cause?
- **Q: Is there an inability to recall important autobiographical information (often stressful/traumatic), too extensive for ordinary forgetting, without a neurological cause?**
  - YES → Dissociative Amnesia — Dissociative Amnesia (differential Table 3.8.1)
  - NO → next question: Does the memory complaint occur only during a mood, anxiety, or psychotic disorder (e.g., poor concentration in depression)?
- **Q: Does the memory complaint occur only during a mood, anxiety, or psychotic disorder (e.g., poor concentration in depression)?**
  - YES → Feature of the underlying disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → Age-related or normal forgetfulness — not a mental disorder

### Tree 2.28 — Cognitive Impairment

_Presenting: Cognitive impairment (deficits in one or more cognitive domains) · Group: Neurocognitive presentations_

Broad cognitive impairment requires separating delirium (acute, fluctuating, with attentional disturbance) from the neurocognitive disorders (acquired decline from a prior level), intellectual disability (onset in the developmental period), and cognitive complaints that are features of other mental disorders (e.g., pseudodementia of depression). Establish onset, course, and whether consciousness is clear.

- **Q: Is there an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness developing over hours to days (delirium)?**
  - YES → Delirium — Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1)
  - NO → next question: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?
- **Q: Is it due to the physiological effects of a substance/medication?**
  - YES → Substance/Medication-Induced Neurocognitive Disorder — Substance/Medication-Induced Neurocognitive Disorder (see Tree 2.26)
  - NO → next question: Do the deficits reflect intellectual and adaptive deficits with onset during the developmental period (not a decline)?
- **Q: Do the deficits reflect intellectual and adaptive deficits with onset during the developmental period (not a decline)?**
  - YES → Intellectual Disability — Intellectual Disability (differential Table 3.1.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there acquired decline from a previous level in ≥1 cognitive domain (memory, executive, attention, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition)?
- **Q: Is there acquired decline from a previous level in ≥1 cognitive domain (memory, executive, attention, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition)?**
  - YES → next question: Do the deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities (vs. preserved independence with greater effort)?
  - NO → next question: Are the cognitive complaints occurring only during another mental disorder (e.g., depression, psychosis)?
- **Q: Do the deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities (vs. preserved independence with greater effort)?**
  - YES → Major Neurocognitive Disorder — Major Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2)
  - NO → Mild Neurocognitive Disorder — Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2)
- **Q: Are the cognitive complaints occurring only during another mental disorder (e.g., depression, psychosis)?**
  - YES → Cognitive symptoms as a feature of the underlying disorder — Major Depressive Disorder (differential Table 3.4.1) (see Tree 2.10)
  - NO → No neurocognitive disorder — reassess or monitor

### Tree 2.29 — Etiological Medical Conditions

_Presenting: Psychiatric symptoms possibly due to another medical condition · Group: Etiological medical presentations_

When a general medical condition is judged to physiologically cause the psychiatric symptoms, DSM-5 provides a family of 'Due to Another Medical Condition' diagnoses selected by the predominant symptom. This tree routes an established medical etiology to the correct disorder by presenting symptom.

- **Q: Is there a fluctuating disturbance of attention/awareness with acute onset (delirium)?**
  - YES → Delirium Due to Another Medical Condition — Delirium (differential Table 3.16.1)
  - NO → next question: Is there acquired cognitive decline (major/mild)?
- **Q: Is there acquired cognitive decline (major/mild)?**
  - YES → Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (differential Table 3.16.2)
  - NO → next question: Are psychotic symptoms (delusions/hallucinations) predominant?
- **Q: Are psychotic symptoms (delusions/hallucinations) predominant?**
  - YES → Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
  - NO → next question: Is elevated/expansive/irritable mood with increased energy predominant?
- **Q: Is elevated/expansive/irritable mood with increased energy predominant?**
  - YES → Bipolar and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Bipolar and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
  - NO → next question: Is depressed mood or anhedonia predominant?
- **Q: Is depressed mood or anhedonia predominant?**
  - YES → Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
  - NO → next question: Is anxiety (panic/worry) predominant?
- **Q: Is anxiety (panic/worry) predominant?**
  - YES → Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
  - NO → next question: Are obsessive-compulsive/related symptoms predominant?
- **Q: Are obsessive-compulsive/related symptoms predominant?**
  - YES → Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — OCD and Related Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
  - NO → next question: Is there a persistent personality change from baseline attributable to the medical condition?
- **Q: Is there a persistent personality change from baseline attributable to the medical condition?**
  - YES → Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition — Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition (differential Table 3.17.11)
  - NO → next question: Are catatonic features predominant?
- **Q: Are catatonic features predominant?**
  - YES → Catatonic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — Catatonic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition (differential Table 3.2.5) (see Tree 2.7)
  - NO → If symptoms don't fit a specific category: Other Specified/Unspecified Mental Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition

## Part 3 — Disorder Differential Tables (66)

### Table 3.1.1 — Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)

**Intellectual Disability:** Deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning with onset during the developmental period.

- **Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Involves acquired decline from a previously attained level of functioning, rather than deficits present from the developmental period.
- **Specific Learning Disorder** (Table 3.1.5) — Deficits are circumscribed to specific academic skills without global impairment of intellectual and adaptive functioning.
- **Communication Disorders / Language Disorder** (Table 3.1.2) — Impairment is limited to communication/language; nonverbal intellectual and adaptive functioning are relatively preserved.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Defined by social-communication deficits and restricted/repetitive behavior; may co-occur with intellectual disability, in which case both are diagnosed.

### Table 3.1.2 — Communication Disorders

**Communication Disorders (Language, Speech Sound, Childhood-Onset Fluency, Social Pragmatic):** Persistent difficulties in language, speech production, fluency, or the social use of communication.

- **Intellectual Disability** (Table 3.1.1) — Communication problems are part of a broader deficit in intellectual and adaptive functioning rather than a circumscribed language/speech deficit.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Includes restricted/repetitive behaviors and interests in addition to social-communication deficits; Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder is diagnosed only when ASD is excluded.
- **Hearing or other sensory impairment** — Communication difficulty is fully attributable to a sensory deficit and resolves/adapts with appropriate accommodation.
- **Selective Mutism** (Table 3.5.2) — Language capacity is intact but the child fails to speak in specific social situations.
- **Normal variation** — Language/speech is within the expected range for developmental level and cultural/linguistic context.

### Table 3.1.3 — Autism Spectrum Disorder

**Autism Spectrum Disorder:** Persistent deficits in social communication/interaction plus restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities, with onset in the early developmental period.

- **Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder** (Table 3.1.2) — Social-communication deficits are present but restricted/repetitive behaviors are absent.
- **Intellectual Disability** (Table 3.1.1) — Social communication is commensurate with developmental level; diagnosed additionally when social-communication deficits exceed developmental level.
- **Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder** (Table 3.1.4) — Attentional/behavioral problems without the qualitative social-communication deficits and restricted interests of ASD (may co-occur).
- **Selective Mutism** (Table 3.5.2) — Early development and social communication are typically normal; failure to speak is situation-specific.
- **Stereotypic Movement Disorder** — Stereotypies occur without the social-communication deficits of ASD.
- **Schizophrenia (childhood onset)** (Table 3.2.1) — Includes a period of delusions/hallucinations and follows a prodrome; ASD lacks positive psychotic symptoms.

### Table 3.1.4 — Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder

**Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder:** Persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, onset before age 12, present in ≥2 settings and impairing functioning.

- **Oppositional Defiant Disorder** (Table 3.14.1) — Resistance to tasks reflects opposition to authority rather than an inability to sustain attention.
- **Specific Learning Disorder** (Table 3.1.5) — Inattentive appearance is limited to academically demanding tasks and stems from skill deficits.
- **Intellectual Disability** (Table 3.1.1) — Symptoms are commensurate with mental age; ADHD diagnosed additionally if inattention/hyperactivity exceed developmental level.
- **Bipolar Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — Inattention/overactivity is episodic and accompanied by mood elevation, whereas ADHD is chronic and non-episodic.
- **Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder** (Table 3.4.4) — Characterized by persistent irritability and severe temper outbursts beyond the impulsivity of ADHD.
- **Anxiety Disorders** (Table 3.5.7) — Inattention is due to worry/rumination rather than distractibility by external stimuli.
- **Substance Use Disorders** (Table 3.15.1) — Inattention began with substance use rather than in childhood.

### Table 3.1.5 — Specific Learning Disorder

**Specific Learning Disorder:** Persistent difficulties learning and using academic skills (reading, writing, or mathematics) below expectation for age, not explained by other conditions.

- **Intellectual Disability** (Table 3.1.1) — Academic difficulties are part of global intellectual and adaptive impairment.
- **Normal academic variation / lack of instruction** — Underperformance is due to inadequate instruction, absence, or environmental factors rather than a learning disorder.
- **Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Represents a decline from previously attained academic skills rather than a developmental difficulty acquiring them.
- **ADHD** (Table 3.1.4) — Poor academic performance stems from inattention; may co-occur with specific learning disorder.
- **Sensory impairment (vision/hearing)** — Learning difficulty is attributable to an uncorrected sensory deficit.

### Table 3.1.6 — Tic Disorders

**Tic Disorders (Tourette's, Persistent Motor/Vocal, Provisional):** Sudden, rapid, recurrent, nonrhythmic motor movements and/or vocalizations with onset before age 18.

- **Other movement disorders (chorea, dystonia, myoclonus)** — Movements are rhythmic or sustained and not suppressible/preceded by a premonitory urge as tics are.
- **Substance/medication-induced movements** — Abnormal movements are temporally related to a substance or medication (e.g., stimulants, neuroleptics).
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Compulsions are goal-directed and preceded by an obsession, whereas complex tics are less purposeful.
- **Stereotypic Movement Disorder** — Movements are rhythmic, fixed, and driven rather than the brief, variable, suppressible pattern of tics.
- **Movements due to another medical condition** — Abnormal movements are attributable to a neurological condition (e.g., Huntington's, postviral).

### Table 3.2.1 — Schizophrenia or Schizophreniform Disorder

**Schizophrenia / Schizophreniform Disorder:** Two or more active-phase symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, negative symptoms) with functional decline; ≥6 months for schizophrenia, 1–6 months for schizophreniform.

- **Schizoaffective Disorder** (Table 3.2.2) — A mood episode is present for the majority of the total illness duration, with ≥2 weeks of psychosis without prominent mood symptoms.
- **Bipolar/Depressive Disorder With Psychotic Features** (Table 3.4.1) — Psychotic symptoms occur exclusively during mood episodes.
- **Delusional Disorder** (Table 3.2.3) — Delusions occur without the other active-phase symptoms and functioning is otherwise relatively preserved.
- **Brief Psychotic Disorder** (Table 3.2.4) — Duration is at least 1 day but less than 1 month with return to premorbid functioning.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder** — Psychosis is attributable to a substance and typically remits with sustained abstinence.
- **Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Psychosis is the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Prominent delusions/hallucinations are absent; social-communication deficits date from early development.
- **Schizotypal Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.3) — Attenuated, subthreshold psychotic-like features without frank psychosis.

### Table 3.2.2 — Schizoaffective Disorder

**Schizoaffective Disorder:** An uninterrupted illness with a major mood episode concurrent with active-phase schizophrenia symptoms, plus ≥2 weeks of delusions/hallucinations without a major mood episode, with mood episodes present for the majority of the illness.

- **Schizophrenia** (Table 3.2.1) — Mood episodes, if present, occupy only a minority of the total active and residual illness.
- **Major Depressive/Bipolar Disorder With Psychotic Features** (Table 3.4.1) — Psychotic symptoms occur exclusively during mood episodes (no ≥2-week period of psychosis without mood symptoms).
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder** — The combined mood and psychotic picture is attributable to a substance.
- **Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Symptoms are the physiological consequence of a medical condition.

### Table 3.2.3 — Delusional Disorder

**Delusional Disorder:** One or more delusions for ≥1 month without other active-phase schizophrenia symptoms and with relatively preserved functioning apart from the delusion's impact.

- **Schizophrenia** (Table 3.2.1) — Prominent hallucinations, disorganized speech, or negative symptoms are present in addition to delusions.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (with absent insight)** (Table 3.6.1) — Beliefs are tied to obsessions/compulsions and were once recognized as excessive.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder (with absent insight)** (Table 3.6.2) — The fixed belief concerns a perceived appearance defect.
- **Depressive/Bipolar Disorder With Psychotic Features** (Table 3.4.1) — Delusions occur only during mood episodes.
- **Paranoid Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.1) — Pervasive suspiciousness without a fixed, circumscribed delusion.
- **Substance-Induced or Medical psychotic disorder** — Delusions are attributable to a substance or medical condition.

### Table 3.2.4 — Brief Psychotic Disorder

**Brief Psychotic Disorder:** Sudden onset of ≥1 positive psychotic symptom lasting at least 1 day but less than 1 month, with eventual full return to premorbid functioning.

- **Schizophreniform Disorder / Schizophrenia** (Table 3.2.1) — Symptoms persist for 1 month or longer.
- **Mood Disorder With Psychotic Features** (Table 3.4.1) — Psychosis occurs in the context of a manic or major depressive episode.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder** — Psychosis is attributable to substance intoxication/withdrawal or medication.
- **Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Psychosis is the physiological consequence of a medical condition (including delirium).
- **Personality Disorders (e.g., borderline, schizotypal)** (Table 3.17.5) — Transient stress-related paranoia/dissociation without a discrete psychotic episode.

### Table 3.2.5 — Unspecified Catatonia

**Catatonia:** A syndrome of psychomotor disturbance (stupor, catalepsy, mutism, negativism, posturing, echophenomena, agitation) occurring in association with another disorder or medical condition.

- **Catatonia Associated With a Mental Disorder** — Occurs during a psychotic, bipolar, or depressive disorder and is specified as such rather than 'unspecified.'
- **Catatonic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Directly caused by a medical condition.
- **Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome** — Medication-induced hyperthermia, rigidity, and autonomic instability requiring urgent medical management.
- **Parkinsonism / other movement disorders** — Motor findings are explained by a defined neurological movement disorder rather than catatonia.

### Table 3.3.1 — Bipolar I Disorder

**Bipolar I Disorder:** At least one Manic Episode (≥1 week or hospitalization, with marked impairment or psychosis), usually with major depressive episodes.

- **Bipolar II Disorder** (Table 3.3.2) — Hypomanic (not full manic) episodes plus major depressive episodes; never a full manic episode.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — No lifetime history of a manic or hypomanic episode.
- **Cyclothymic Disorder** (Table 3.3.3) — Chronic subthreshold hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria.
- **Schizoaffective Disorder** (Table 3.2.2) — ≥2 weeks of psychosis occur without a mood episode.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar Disorder** — Mood elevation is attributable to a substance/medication and does not persist beyond its physiological effect.
- **Bipolar Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Mania is the direct physiological consequence of a medical condition.
- **ADHD** (Table 3.1.4) — Chronic, non-episodic overactivity/impulsivity without discrete mood episodes (may co-occur, especially in youth).
- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Mood shifts are rapid, reactive to interpersonal events, and lack sustained episodes with increased energy/decreased sleep.

### Table 3.3.2 — Bipolar II Disorder

**Bipolar II Disorder:** At least one Hypomanic Episode and at least one Major Depressive Episode, with no history of a full Manic Episode.

- **Bipolar I Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — A full manic episode has occurred at some point.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — No lifetime hypomanic episode.
- **Cyclothymic Disorder** (Table 3.3.3) — Symptoms never reach full hypomanic or major depressive episode thresholds.
- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Affective instability is brief and reactive rather than sustained hypomanic episodes.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar Disorder** — Hypomania is attributable to a substance and does not persist with abstinence.

### Table 3.3.3 — Cyclothymic Disorder

**Cyclothymic Disorder:** ≥2 years (1 year in youth) of numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria, present at least half the time.

- **Bipolar I/II Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — Full manic, hypomanic, or major depressive episodes have occurred.
- **Bipolar Disorder, rapid cycling** — Discrete full episodes recur frequently, unlike the chronic subthreshold fluctuation of cyclothymia.
- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Mood instability is reactive and tied to interpersonal stressors and identity disturbance.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced or Medical mood disorder** — Mood fluctuations are attributable to a substance or medical condition.

### Table 3.4.1 — Major Depressive Disorder

**Major Depressive Disorder:** One or more Major Depressive Episodes (≥2 weeks of depressed mood/anhedonia plus additional symptoms) without a history of mania/hypomania.

- **Bipolar I/II Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — A lifetime manic or hypomanic episode has occurred.
- **Persistent Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.2) — Chronic depressed mood for ≥2 years, often at lower symptom intensity.
- **Adjustment Disorder With Depressed Mood** (Table 3.7.2) — Depressive symptoms follow a stressor and do not meet full major depressive episode criteria.
- **Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Depression is the direct physiological result of a medical condition.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder** — Depression is attributable to a substance/medication.
- **Schizoaffective Disorder** (Table 3.2.2) — Psychosis occurs for ≥2 weeks outside of mood episodes.
- **Uncomplicated bereavement / normal sadness** — A normative reaction to loss that does not reflect an internal dysfunction (clinical judgment required).
- **ADHD / Demoralization** (Table 3.1.4) — Poor concentration or low mood is secondary to functional impairment without a full depressive syndrome.

### Table 3.4.2 — Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

**Persistent Depressive Disorder:** Depressed mood most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (≥1 year in youth), with additional depressive symptoms and no symptom-free interval longer than 2 months.

- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Episodic rather than chronic; MDD and persistent depressive disorder may be diagnosed together when criteria for both are met.
- **Bipolar Disorders / Cyclothymic Disorder** (Table 3.3.3) — History of hypomanic/manic symptoms.
- **Psychotic disorders (residual)** (Table 3.2.1) — Chronic low mood occurs within a primary psychotic illness.
- **Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition / Substance-Induced** — Chronic depression is attributable to a medical condition or substance.
- **Personality Disorders** — Enduring maladaptive traits without pervasive depressed mood (may co-occur).

### Table 3.4.3 — Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder

**Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder:** Mood and physical symptoms in the final premenstrual week that improve within a few days of menses onset, confirmed by prospective ratings.

- **Premenstrual exacerbation of another disorder** — An underlying disorder (e.g., MDD) is present throughout the cycle and merely worsens premenstrually.
- **Dysmenorrhea** — Painful menses without the characteristic mood symptom cluster.
- **Major Depressive Disorder / Persistent Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Mood symptoms are not confined to the luteal phase.
- **Effects of hormonal treatment** — Symptoms are attributable to exogenous hormones.

### Table 3.4.4 — Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder

**Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder:** Severe recurrent temper outbursts with persistently irritable/angry mood between outbursts, onset before age 10, diagnosed between ages 6 and 18.

- **Bipolar Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — Irritability is episodic with distinct periods of mood elevation/increased energy, not chronic.
- **Oppositional Defiant Disorder** (Table 3.14.1) — DMDD requires severe outbursts and persistent inter-outburst irritability; when both sets of criteria are met, only DMDD is diagnosed.
- **Intermittent Explosive Disorder** (Table 3.14.2) — Lacks the persistent negative mood between outbursts that characterizes DMDD.
- **ADHD / Conduct Disorder** (Table 3.1.4) — May co-occur; distinguished by the chronic irritable mood core of DMDD.
- **Major Depressive Disorder / Anxiety Disorders** (Table 3.4.1) — If full criteria for DMDD and MDD are both met, DMDD is not additionally diagnosed.

### Table 3.5.1 — Separation Anxiety Disorder

**Separation Anxiety Disorder:** Developmentally inappropriate, excessive fear/anxiety about separation from attachment figures.

- **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.7) — Worry spans many domains rather than focusing on separation.
- **Panic Disorder / Agoraphobia** (Table 3.5.5) — Fear centers on panic symptoms or escape difficulty rather than separation from attachment figures.
- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Avoidance is due to fear of scrutiny, not separation.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — School/social refusal relates to sensory/social intolerance rather than separation fear.
- **Conduct Disorder / Truancy** (Table 3.14.3) — School avoidance is to pursue activities outside home rather than to stay near attachment figures.
- **Illness Anxiety / PTSD** (Table 3.7.1) — Fear is focused on illness or trauma reminders rather than separation.

### Table 3.5.2 — Selective Mutism

**Selective Mutism:** Consistent failure to speak in specific social situations where speaking is expected, despite speaking in others, interfering with functioning.

- **Communication Disorders** (Table 3.1.2) — Speech difficulty is not restricted to specific social situations.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Failure to speak reflects social-communication deficits present across settings.
- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Broader social fear; selective mutism specifically involves not speaking and may be diagnosed alongside it.
- **Normal shyness / adjustment (e.g., new language)** — Transient silence during adjustment to a new environment or language does not warrant the diagnosis.

### Table 3.5.3 — Specific Phobia

**Specific Phobia:** Marked fear/anxiety about a specific object or situation, actively avoided, out of proportion to actual danger.

- **Agoraphobia** (Table 3.5.6) — Fear spans multiple situations centered on escape/help difficulty rather than one circumscribed object.
- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Fear is of social scrutiny.
- **Separation Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.1) — Avoidance relates to separation from attachment figures.
- **PTSD** (Table 3.7.1) — Avoidance is of trauma reminders.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Avoidance is driven by obsessions (e.g., contamination).
- **Panic Disorder** (Table 3.5.5) — Panic attacks are unexpected rather than cued only by a specific phobic stimulus.

### Table 3.5.4 — Social Anxiety Disorder (Social Phobia)

**Social Anxiety Disorder:** Marked fear/anxiety about social situations involving possible scrutiny, with fear of negative evaluation.

- **Panic Disorder** (Table 3.5.5) — Unexpected panic attacks not limited to social situations.
- **Agoraphobia** (Table 3.5.6) — Fear is of escape difficulty/incapacitation rather than scrutiny.
- **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.7) — Worry extends to nonsocial domains and ongoing relationships.
- **Separation Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.1) — Discomfort persists even with attachment figures present in social settings.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Social-communication deficits with lack of age-appropriate relationships; social anxiety patients retain social capacity.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Social avoidance stems from beliefs about appearance flaws.
- **Avoidant Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.8) — Pervasive; frequently overlaps and both may be diagnosed.
- **Normal performance anxiety / shyness** — Not markedly impairing or distressing.

### Table 3.5.5 — Panic Disorder

**Panic Disorder:** Recurrent unexpected panic attacks with ≥1 month of concern about further attacks or maladaptive behavior change.

- **Other anxiety disorders with cued attacks** (Table 3.5.4) — Panic attacks are expected (cued by phobic, social, or trauma stimuli) rather than unexpected.
- **Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition** — Panic is caused by a medical condition (e.g., arrhythmia, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma).
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder** — Panic attributable to a substance (e.g., caffeine, stimulants) or withdrawal.
- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Preoccupation with having a serious illness without recurrent unexpected panic.
- **Agoraphobia** (Table 3.5.6) — May co-occur; agoraphobia is coded separately when present.

### Table 3.5.6 — Agoraphobia

**Agoraphobia:** Marked fear/avoidance of ≥2 situations (public transport, open/enclosed spaces, crowds, being outside alone) due to fear that escape or help may be unavailable.

- **Specific Phobia, situational** (Table 3.5.3) — Fear involves a single situation rather than the ≥2 agoraphobic clusters.
- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Avoidance is due to fear of scrutiny.
- **Separation Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.1) — Avoidance relates to separation from attachment figures.
- **PTSD** (Table 3.7.1) — Avoidance is of trauma reminders.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Reduced going out reflects anhedonia/amotivation rather than fear of escape difficulty.

### Table 3.5.7 — Generalized Anxiety Disorder

**Generalized Anxiety Disorder:** Excessive, hard-to-control worry about multiple domains, more days than not for ≥6 months, with somatic symptoms.

- **Anxiety Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition / Substance-Induced** — Anxiety is attributable to a medical condition or substance.
- **Social Anxiety / Panic / Specific Phobia** (Table 3.5.4) — Worry is confined to a specific focus rather than generalized.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Anxiety is driven by intrusive obsessions rather than realistic-themed worry.
- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Worry centers specifically on having a serious illness.
- **Major Depressive Disorder / Adjustment Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Worry is confined to a depressive episode or a stressor-related adjustment reaction.
- **Normal worry** — Worry is controllable, not markedly distressing, and not accompanied by the somatic symptom burden.

### Table 3.6.1 — Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

**Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder:** Recurrent obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming or cause significant distress/impairment.

- **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.7) — Excessive worry about real-life concerns rather than intrusive, ego-dystonic obsessions with rituals.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Preoccupations and rituals focus specifically on perceived appearance defects.
- **Hoarding Disorder** (Table 3.6.3) — Difficulty discarding driven by perceived need to save, not by obsessions.
- **Trichotillomania / Excoriation** (Table 3.6.4) — Body-focused repetitive behaviors without preceding obsessions.
- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Preoccupation is specifically about having an illness.
- **Tic Disorders** (Table 3.1.6) — Tics are not preceded by an obsession and are less purposeful.
- **Psychotic disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — OCD retains at least some insight in most cases; delusional disorder involves fixed non-ritualized beliefs.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.10) — Pervasive perfectionism/control as ego-syntonic traits without true obsessions/compulsions.

### Table 3.6.2 — Body Dysmorphic Disorder

**Body Dysmorphic Disorder:** Preoccupation with perceived defects/flaws in appearance not observable or slight to others, with repetitive behaviors.

- **Normal appearance concerns** — Not preoccupying, distressing, or impairing.
- **Eating Disorders** (Table 3.10.2) — Concern is specifically about body weight/shape and eating behavior.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Obsessions/compulsions are not limited to appearance.
- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Fear of scrutiny is not based on a specific perceived appearance defect.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Appearance-related negative thoughts occur only within a depressive episode.
- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Preoccupation is with illness rather than appearance.

### Table 3.6.3 — Hoarding Disorder

**Hoarding Disorder:** Persistent difficulty discarding possessions due to a perceived need to save, resulting in clutter that compromises living areas.

- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Accumulation results from obsessions (e.g., contamination) and is unwanted, not driven by a desire to save.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Clutter reflects psychomotor slowing/amotivation rather than saving urges.
- **Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Accumulation stems from cognitive decline.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder / Psychotic disorders** (Table 3.1.3) — Collecting is tied to restricted interests or delusional beliefs.
- **Normal collecting** — Organized, valued collections without clutter or impairment.

### Table 3.6.4 — Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder)

**Trichotillomania:** Recurrent pulling out of one's hair causing hair loss, with repeated attempts to stop.

- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Hair pulling is not performed in response to an obsession.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Hair removal is to correct a perceived appearance defect.
- **Stereotypic Movement Disorder / Tics** (Table 3.1.6) — Movements are stereotyped or tic-like rather than focused hair pulling.
- **Psychotic disorder** (Table 3.2.1) — Pulling responds to a delusion or hallucination.
- **Dermatological/medical hair loss** — Alopecia is due to a medical condition rather than pulling.

### Table 3.6.5 — Excoriation (Skin-Picking) Disorder

**Excoriation Disorder:** Recurrent skin picking causing lesions, with repeated attempts to stop.

- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Picking is not driven by an obsession.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Picking is to improve a perceived appearance defect.
- **Non-Suicidal Self-Injury** — Intent is to relieve negative feelings via self-harm rather than habitual picking.
- **Psychotic disorder (e.g., delusional parasitosis)** (Table 3.2.3) — Picking responds to a delusion of infestation.
- **Substance-induced (e.g., stimulants)** — Picking occurs during stimulant use (formication).
- **Dermatological condition** — Skin lesions are due to a primary dermatologic disorder.

### Table 3.7.1 — Posttraumatic Stress Disorder or Acute Stress Disorder

**PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder:** Intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood alterations, and arousal following exposure to actual/threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence (>1 month = PTSD; 3 days–1 month = ASD).

- **Adjustment Disorder** (Table 3.7.2) — Stressor is not of the required traumatic type, or the response does not meet the PTSD symptom pattern.
- **Anxiety Disorders / OCD** (Table 3.5.7) — Intrusive thoughts are not tied to a traumatic event.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Lacks the intrusion and trauma-related avoidance symptoms.
- **Dissociative Disorders** (Table 3.8.2) — Dissociation occurs without the full PTSD symptom picture.
- **Psychotic Disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — Flashbacks are trauma-linked reexperiencing, not primary hallucinations/delusions.
- **Traumatic brain injury** — Neurocognitive symptoms are due to the physical injury rather than the psychological trauma.

### Table 3.7.2 — Adjustment Disorder

**Adjustment Disorder:** Clinically significant emotional/behavioral symptoms developing within 3 months of an identifiable stressor, resolving within 6 months of its termination, not meeting criteria for another disorder.

- **Major Depressive Disorder / Anxiety Disorders** (Table 3.4.1) — Full criteria for a specific disorder are met; adjustment disorder is a residual diagnosis.
- **PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder** (Table 3.7.1) — The stressor is traumatic and the characteristic symptom pattern is present.
- **Personality Disorders** — Symptoms are enduring traits; adjustment disorder may be diagnosed additionally if a stressor causes acute worsening.
- **Normal stress reaction / uncomplicated bereavement** — Response is within expectable/culturally normative limits and not markedly impairing.

### Table 3.8.1 — Dissociative Amnesia

**Dissociative Amnesia:** Inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic/stressful nature, inconsistent with ordinary forgetting.

- **Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Memory loss is part of a broader cognitive decline with anterograde impairment.
- **Substance-Induced (e.g., alcohol blackout)** — Amnesia is for events during intoxication and attributable to the substance.
- **Posttraumatic Stress Disorder** (Table 3.7.1) — Amnesia is limited to a component of the trauma within the full PTSD picture.
- **Amnesia due to another medical condition (e.g., seizure, TBI)** — Memory loss is the physiological consequence of a medical/neurological event.
- **Malingering / Factitious Disorder** (Table 3.9.5) — Amnesia is feigned for external incentive or to assume the sick role.

### Table 3.8.2 — Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder

**Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder:** Persistent/recurrent experiences of detachment from one's self (depersonalization) or surroundings (derealization) with intact reality testing.

- **Panic Disorder / Anxiety Disorders** (Table 3.5.5) — Depersonalization occurs only during panic attacks or anxiety episodes.
- **PTSD / Acute Stress Disorder** (Table 3.7.1) — Dissociation is part of the trauma-related symptom picture.
- **Psychotic Disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — In psychosis, reality testing is lost; here the individual knows the experience is not real.
- **Substance-Induced / Medical** — Symptoms are attributable to a substance (e.g., cannabis, hallucinogens) or medical condition (e.g., seizures).
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Detachment occurs only within a depressive episode.

### Table 3.9.1 — Somatic Symptom Disorder

**Somatic Symptom Disorder:** One or more distressing somatic symptoms plus excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors related to them.

- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Somatic symptoms are minimal/absent; the focus is fear of having/acquiring illness.
- **Other medical condition** — A medical condition explains the symptoms and the psychological response is proportionate.
- **Panic Disorder** (Table 3.5.5) — Somatic symptoms are episodic and confined to panic attacks.
- **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.7) — Worry is generalized rather than focused on somatic symptoms.
- **Conversion Disorder** (Table 3.9.3) — Symptoms are specifically neurological and incompatible with recognized disease.
- **Depressive Disorders** (Table 3.4.1) — Somatic complaints occur only within a depressive episode.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Preoccupation is with appearance rather than somatic symptoms.

### Table 3.9.2 — Illness Anxiety Disorder

**Illness Anxiety Disorder:** Preoccupation with having/acquiring a serious illness, with minimal somatic symptoms and high health-related anxiety.

- **Somatic Symptom Disorder** (Table 3.9.1) — Prominent distressing somatic symptoms are present.
- **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.7) — Worry spans many topics, not just illness.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Illness-related intrusive thoughts are obsessions with associated compulsions across other themes.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Health preoccupation occurs only within a depressive episode.
- **Delusional Disorder, somatic type** (Table 3.2.3) — The illness belief is held with delusional conviction.
- **Adjustment reaction to real medical illness** — Anxiety is a proportionate response to a genuine, serious diagnosis.

### Table 3.9.3 — Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder)

**Conversion Disorder:** Altered voluntary motor or sensory function with clinical findings incompatible with recognized neurological/medical conditions.

- **Neurological disease** — Examination shows findings compatible with a recognized neurological disorder.
- **Somatic Symptom Disorder** (Table 3.9.1) — Symptoms are not specifically neurological or incompatibility is not demonstrable.
- **Factitious Disorder / Malingering** (Table 3.9.5) — Symptoms are intentionally produced (for the sick role or external incentive).
- **Dissociative Disorders** (Table 3.8.1) — Symptoms are dissociative (memory/identity) rather than sensorimotor.
- **Panic Disorder** (Table 3.5.5) — Neurological-seeming symptoms occur only during panic attacks.

### Table 3.9.4 — Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions

**Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions:** Psychological/behavioral factors that adversely affect a documented medical condition by influencing its course, treatment, or pathophysiology.

- **Mental disorder with a medical cause** — There a psychiatric disorder is caused by the medical condition, not psychological factors worsening it.
- **Adjustment Disorder** (Table 3.7.2) — A psychological response to the medical illness that itself meets disorder criteria, rather than factors worsening the illness.
- **Somatic Symptom Disorder** (Table 3.9.1) — Excessive thoughts/behaviors about symptoms dominate rather than measurable worsening of a medical condition.
- **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.9.2) — Health anxiety predominates without a medical condition being worsened.

### Table 3.9.5 — Factitious Disorder

**Factitious Disorder:** Falsification of physical/psychological signs or symptoms, or induction of injury/disease, in the absence of obvious external rewards.

- **Malingering** — Feigning is motivated by a tangible external incentive (money, drugs, avoiding duties).
- **Somatic Symptom Disorder** (Table 3.9.1) — Symptoms are genuinely experienced rather than deliberately falsified.
- **Conversion Disorder** (Table 3.9.3) — Symptoms are not intentionally produced.
- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Self-harm is to relieve distress rather than to deceive and assume the sick role.
- **Genuine medical condition** — Signs are authentic and not induced or falsified.

### Table 3.10.1 — Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder

**Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder:** Avoidance/restriction of food intake with nutritional/energy or psychosocial consequences, not driven by body-image concerns.

- **Anorexia Nervosa** (Table 3.10.2) — Restriction is driven by fear of weight gain and body-image disturbance.
- **Medical condition (e.g., GI disease, food allergy)** — Restriction is fully accounted for by a medical condition.
- **Major Depressive Disorder / Anxiety** (Table 3.4.1) — Reduced intake is secondary to appetite loss within another disorder.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Selective eating is part of restricted/sensory features; ARFID diagnosed additionally if severe.
- **Normal picky eating** — Does not cause nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements, or psychosocial impairment.

### Table 3.10.2 — Anorexia Nervosa

**Anorexia Nervosa:** Restriction of intake leading to significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain, and disturbance in experience of body weight/shape.

- **Bulimia Nervosa** (Table 3.10.3) — Body weight is maintained at or above normal despite binge-purge cycles.
- **Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder** (Table 3.10.1) — Low weight without body-image disturbance or fear of weight gain.
- **Medical causes of low weight** — Weight loss is due to a medical illness without the characteristic body-image disturbance.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Weight loss from appetite reduction without fear of weight gain or body-image disturbance.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Preoccupation concerns appearance features other than weight/shape.
- **Psychotic disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — Food refusal is due to delusions (e.g., of poisoning) rather than weight concern.

### Table 3.10.3 — Bulimia Nervosa

**Bulimia Nervosa:** Recurrent binge eating with inappropriate compensatory behaviors, ≥once weekly for 3 months, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape/weight.

- **Anorexia Nervosa, binge-eating/purging type** (Table 3.10.2) — The individual is at significantly low weight; anorexia takes precedence.
- **Binge-Eating Disorder** (Table 3.10.4) — Binges occur without regular compensatory behaviors.
- **Major Depressive Disorder with atypical features** (Table 3.4.1) — Overeating occurs without binge loss-of-control or compensation.
- **Kleptomania / impulse-control disorders** — Impulsivity is not centered on binge eating.
- **Medical causes of overeating (e.g., Kleine-Levin)** — Hyperphagia is attributable to a neurological/medical syndrome.

### Table 3.10.4 — Binge-Eating Disorder

**Binge-Eating Disorder:** Recurrent binge eating with marked distress, without the regular compensatory behaviors of bulimia nervosa.

- **Bulimia Nervosa** (Table 3.10.3) — Regular inappropriate compensatory behaviors are present.
- **Obesity (without binge eating)** — Overeating occurs without discrete binge episodes with loss of control.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Increased appetite without discrete binges with loss of control.
- **Bipolar Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — Overeating occurs within mood episodes.

### Table 3.11.1 — Insomnia Disorder

**Insomnia Disorder:** Dissatisfaction with sleep quantity/quality (difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep or early awakening) ≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months with daytime impairment.

- **Normal sleep variation / situational insomnia** — Short-lived or does not cause significant daytime impairment.
- **Another sleep-wake disorder** — Insomnia is explained by a breathing-related, circadian rhythm, or restless legs disorder.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder** — Attributable to a substance, medication, or caffeine.
- **Mental disorders (MDD, GAD)** (Table 3.4.1) — Insomnia is fully accounted for by another disorder; insomnia disorder is added when it is an independent focus.
- **Sleep disturbance due to another medical condition** — Caused by pain, nocturia, or other medical factors.

### Table 3.11.2 — Hypersomnolence Disorder

**Hypersomnolence Disorder:** Excessive sleepiness despite ≥7 hours of sleep, with prolonged sleep, difficulty being fully awake, or nonrestorative sleep, ≥3 times/week for ≥3 months.

- **Narcolepsy** — Presence of cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and characteristic REM findings.
- **Breathing-Related Sleep Disorder** — Sleepiness is due to sleep-disordered breathing (e.g., obstructive apnea).
- **Insufficient sleep** — Sleepiness resolves with adequate sleep opportunity.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Sleep Disorder** — Attributable to sedating substances or medications.
- **Major Depressive Disorder with hypersomnia** (Table 3.4.1) — Excessive sleep occurs only within a depressive episode.

### Table 3.12.1 — Sexual Dysfunctions

**Sexual Dysfunctions:** Clinically significant disturbance in sexual response or pleasure, typically ≥6 months, causing distress.

- **Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction** — Dysfunction is attributable to a substance or medication (e.g., SSRIs, antihypertensives).
- **Sexual dysfunction due to another medical condition** — Fully explained by a medical condition (vascular, neurologic, endocrine).
- **Another nonsexual mental disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Dysfunction is better explained by another disorder (e.g., major depressive disorder).
- **Severe relationship distress or other stressors** — Dysfunction is attributable to relationship problems, partner factors, or contextual stressors.
- **Normal variation / inadequate stimulation** — Response is within normal limits given age, stimulation, and context, and is not distressing.

### Table 3.13.1 — Gender Dysphoria

**Gender Dysphoria:** Marked incongruence between one's experienced/expressed gender and assigned gender, with clinically significant distress or impairment.

- **Nonconformity to gender roles** — Gender-atypical behavior without the marked incongruence and distress required for the diagnosis.
- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder** (Table 3.6.2) — Preoccupation is with a perceived appearance defect rather than gender incongruence.
- **Transvestic Disorder** (Table 3.18.1) — Cross-dressing is for sexual arousal without a desire to be the other gender.
- **Psychotic disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — Any belief about being another gender is delusional rather than an identity experience.

### Table 3.14.1 — Oppositional Defiant Disorder

**Oppositional Defiant Disorder:** A pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behavior, or vindictiveness lasting ≥6 months.

- **Conduct Disorder** (Table 3.14.3) — Involves violation of others' basic rights and major societal norms, beyond defiance.
- **Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder** (Table 3.4.4) — Severe temper outbursts plus persistent irritability; when both are met, DMDD is diagnosed instead.
- **ADHD** (Table 3.1.4) — Noncompliance stems from inattention/impulsivity rather than deliberate defiance (often co-occurs).
- **Depressive/Bipolar Disorders** (Table 3.4.1) — Oppositional behavior occurs only during mood episodes.
- **Intermittent Explosive Disorder** (Table 3.14.2) — Characterized by serious aggressive outbursts rather than a broad defiant pattern.
- **Normal developmental oppositionality** — Behavior is within expectable frequency/intensity for developmental stage.

### Table 3.14.2 — Intermittent Explosive Disorder

**Intermittent Explosive Disorder:** Recurrent impulsive/anger-based aggressive outbursts grossly out of proportion to provocation, not premeditated.

- **Conduct Disorder / Antisocial Personality Disorder** (Table 3.14.3) — Aggression is instrumental/premeditated and part of a broader antisocial pattern.
- **Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder** (Table 3.4.4) — Persistent irritable mood between outbursts; DMDD takes precedence in youth when both are met.
- **Bipolar Disorder** (Table 3.3.1) — Outbursts occur during manic episodes.
- **Substance Intoxication/Withdrawal** (Table 3.15.1) — Aggression occurs in the context of substance use.
- **Personality Disorders (borderline, antisocial)** (Table 3.17.5) — Aggression is part of a pervasive personality pattern.
- **Aggression due to another medical condition** — Outbursts are the physiological consequence of a medical condition (e.g., head trauma).

### Table 3.14.3 — Conduct Disorder

**Conduct Disorder:** A repetitive, persistent pattern of behavior violating others' basic rights or major age-appropriate societal norms.

- **Oppositional Defiant Disorder** (Table 3.14.1) — Defiance and irritability without violation of others' basic rights.
- **Antisocial Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.4) — The adult (≥18) continuation; conduct disorder is required before age 15.
- **ADHD** (Table 3.1.4) — Impulsive rule-breaking without a sustained pattern of rights violations (often co-occurs).
- **Bipolar/Depressive Disorders** (Table 3.3.1) — Antisocial behavior occurs only during mood episodes.
- **Intermittent Explosive Disorder** (Table 3.14.2) — Aggression is impulsive outbursts without the broader pattern of nonaggressive conduct violations.
- **Adjustment Disorder** (Table 3.7.2) — Conduct disturbance is a time-limited reaction to a stressor.

### Table 3.15.1 — Substance Use Disorders

**Substance Use Disorders:** A problematic pattern of substance use leading to clinically significant impairment/distress, with impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological criteria.

- **Nonpathological substance use** — Use without impaired control, compulsion, or the required impairment criteria.
- **Substance-Induced Mental Disorders** — Refers to the psychiatric syndromes caused by the substance rather than the use pattern itself.
- **Gambling Disorder** (Table 3.15.2) — A non-substance (behavioral) addiction sharing features but not involving substance ingestion.
- **Primary mental disorder with secondary use** — Use is self-medication of a primary disorder present during abstinent periods.

### Table 3.15.2 — Gambling Disorder

**Gambling Disorder:** Persistent, recurrent problematic gambling leading to clinically significant impairment or distress.

- **Professional/social gambling** — Gambling is disciplined, limited, and not associated with loss of control or impairment.
- **Manic Episode** (Table 3.3.1) — Excessive gambling occurs only during mood elevation.
- **Antisocial Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.4) — Gambling is part of a broader antisocial pattern (may co-occur).
- **Substance Use Disorders** (Table 3.15.1) — Shares addictive features but involves substance ingestion.

### Table 3.16.1 — Delirium

**Delirium:** An acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with an additional cognitive disturbance, developing over hours to days from a medical cause, substance, or multiple etiologies.

- **Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Chronic, stable cognitive decline with preserved attention/awareness (though delirium can be superimposed).
- **Psychotic Disorders** (Table 3.2.1) — Consciousness/attention are intact and onset is not acute-fluctuating.
- **Acute stress / dissociative states** (Table 3.8.2) — No global attentional disturbance with a medical/substance cause.
- **Major Depressive Disorder (with poor concentration)** (Table 3.4.1) — Attention/awareness are not acutely and globally impaired with fluctuation.

### Table 3.16.2 — Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder

**Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder:** Acquired decline from a previous level in ≥1 cognitive domain; major NCD interferes with independence, mild NCD does not.

- **Delirium** (Table 3.16.1) — Acute, fluctuating attentional disturbance rather than a stable decline.
- **Intellectual Disability** (Table 3.1.1) — Deficits are developmental and lifelong, not an acquired decline.
- **Major Depressive Disorder (pseudodementia)** (Table 3.4.1) — Cognitive complaints improve with treatment of the depressive episode.
- **Normal aging** — Age-expected cognitive change without significant decline or impairment.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced Neurocognitive Disorder** — Decline is attributable to a substance/medication.
- **Schizophrenia** (Table 3.2.1) — Cognitive impairment occurs within a primary psychotic illness.

### Table 3.17.1 — Paranoid Personality Disorder

**Paranoid Personality Disorder:** Pervasive distrust and suspiciousness such that others' motives are interpreted as malevolent, beginning by early adulthood.

- **Delusional Disorder, persecutory type** (Table 3.2.3) — Involves fixed delusions rather than pervasive but non-delusional suspiciousness.
- **Schizophrenia** (Table 3.2.1) — Includes frank psychotic symptoms.
- **Schizotypal Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.3) — Adds cognitive-perceptual distortions and eccentricity.
- **Schizoid Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.2) — Detachment without prominent suspiciousness.
- **Personality change due to another medical condition / substance-related** (Table 3.17.11) — Suspiciousness is attributable to a medical condition or substance.

### Table 3.17.2 — Schizoid Personality Disorder

**Schizoid Personality Disorder:** Pervasive detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of emotional expression.

- **Schizotypal Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.3) — Adds cognitive-perceptual distortions and eccentric behavior.
- **Avoidant Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.8) — Social withdrawal stems from fear of rejection, with a desire for closeness.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Includes restricted/repetitive behaviors and early developmental social-communication deficits.
- **Schizophrenia (residual/negative)** (Table 3.2.1) — History of active-phase psychotic symptoms.
- **Major Depressive Disorder** (Table 3.4.1) — Social withdrawal is episodic and mood-linked.

### Table 3.17.3 — Schizotypal Personality Disorder

**Schizotypal Personality Disorder:** Pervasive social/interpersonal deficits with acute discomfort in close relationships, plus cognitive-perceptual distortions and eccentric behavior.

- **Schizophrenia / Delusional Disorder** (Table 3.2.1) — Involves frank, sustained psychotic symptoms rather than attenuated ones.
- **Schizoid/Paranoid Personality Disorders** (Table 3.17.2) — Lack the cognitive-perceptual distortions and eccentricity of schizotypal.
- **Autism Spectrum Disorder** (Table 3.1.3) — Social deficits stem from early developmental communication impairment and restricted interests.
- **Psychotic Disorder Due to Substance/Medical** — Attenuated psychotic-like features are attributable to a substance or medical condition.

### Table 3.17.4 — Antisocial Personality Disorder

**Antisocial Personality Disorder:** A pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of others' rights since age 15, in an individual ≥18 with evidence of conduct disorder before age 15.

- **Conduct Disorder** (Table 3.14.3) — The youth (<18) precursor; antisocial personality disorder requires age ≥18.
- **Substance Use Disorders** (Table 3.15.1) — Antisocial acts occur only in service of substance use, without a lifelong pattern (may co-occur).
- **Narcissistic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.7) — Exploitation without the impulsivity, aggression, and deceit for personal gain seen in antisocial.
- **Manic Episode** (Table 3.3.1) — Irresponsible/antisocial behavior occurs only during mood elevation.
- **Criminal behavior without personality disorder** — Illegal acts occur without the pervasive personality pattern.

### Table 3.17.5 — Borderline Personality Disorder

**Borderline Personality Disorder:** A pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, and affect, with marked impulsivity.

- **Bipolar II / Cyclothymic Disorder** (Table 3.3.2) — Mood shifts are sustained episodes with increased energy/decreased sleep, versus brief reactive shifts.
- **Depressive Disorders** (Table 3.4.1) — Chronic emptiness/instability is a personality trait rather than a discrete episode.
- **Histrionic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.6) — Attention-seeking without the self-destructiveness, emptiness, and rage of borderline.
- **Narcissistic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.7) — Self-image is grandiose and stable rather than unstable.
- **PTSD / Complex trauma** (Table 3.7.1) — Instability is tied to trauma reexperiencing rather than a pervasive personality pattern.
- **Substance-Induced changes** (Table 3.15.1) — Impulsivity/instability occur only during substance use.

### Table 3.17.6 — Histrionic Personality Disorder

**Histrionic Personality Disorder:** A pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking.

- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Adds self-destructiveness, chronic emptiness, and identity disturbance.
- **Narcissistic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.7) — Attention seeking is to affirm superiority rather than for nurturance.
- **Dependent Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.9) — Reliance on others without the dramatic, seductive attention seeking.
- **Somatic Symptom Disorder** (Table 3.9.1) — Focus is on physical symptoms rather than dramatic interpersonal style.

### Table 3.17.7 — Narcissistic Personality Disorder

**Narcissistic Personality Disorder:** A pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy.

- **Antisocial Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.4) — Exploitation is accompanied by impulsivity, aggression, and deceit for material gain.
- **Histrionic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.6) — Attention seeking is for nurturance rather than to confirm superiority.
- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Self-image is unstable rather than grandiose.
- **Manic Episode** (Table 3.3.1) — Grandiosity is episodic and accompanied by mood/energy change.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.10) — Preoccupation with order/perfectionism rather than admiration and grandiosity.

### Table 3.17.8 — Avoidant Personality Disorder

**Avoidant Personality Disorder:** A pervasive pattern of social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation.

- **Social Anxiety Disorder** (Table 3.5.4) — Largely overlapping; both may be diagnosed. Personality disorder is broader and earlier-onset.
- **Schizoid Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.2) — Detachment reflects lack of desire for relationships, not fear of rejection.
- **Dependent Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.9) — Focus is on being cared for rather than on avoiding humiliation.
- **Agoraphobia** (Table 3.5.6) — Avoidance is of situations where escape is difficult rather than of social scrutiny.

### Table 3.17.9 — Dependent Personality Disorder

**Dependent Personality Disorder:** A pervasive and excessive need to be taken care of, leading to submissive/clinging behavior and fears of separation.

- **Borderline Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.5) — Reacts to abandonment with rage/emptiness rather than submission and seeking a new caretaker.
- **Histrionic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.6) — Uses gregarious, demanding attention seeking rather than submissive dependence.
- **Avoidant Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.8) — Withdraws from relationships until certain of acceptance, rather than clinging.
- **Dependence due to a medical condition / mood disorder** — Dependent behavior is secondary to another disorder.

### Table 3.17.10 — Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder

**Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder:** A pervasive preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism, and mental/interpersonal control at the expense of flexibility and efficiency.

- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Presence of true obsessions and compulsions, whereas OCPD involves ego-syntonic traits without them.
- **Hoarding Disorder** (Table 3.6.3) — Clinically significant hoarding may warrant a separate diagnosis.
- **Narcissistic Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.7) — Perfectionism serves self-image of superiority rather than orderliness/control for its own sake.
- **Personality change due to another medical condition** (Table 3.17.11) — Traits are an acquired change attributable to a medical condition.

### Table 3.17.11 — Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition

**Personality Change Due to Another Medical Condition:** A persistent personality disturbance representing a change from the individual's previous characteristic pattern, caused by the physiological effects of a medical condition.

- **Primary Personality Disorders** (Table 3.17.5) — Lifelong pattern rather than an acquired change from a medical cause.
- **Delirium / Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.1) — Personality change occurs within a broader disturbance of consciousness or cognition.
- **Substance/Medication-Induced changes** — Change is attributable to a substance/medication rather than a medical condition.
- **Other mental disorders due to a medical condition** — Presentation is dominated by mood, psychosis, or anxiety rather than personality change.

### Table 3.18.1 — Paraphilic Disorders

**Paraphilic Disorders:** Recurrent, intense atypical sexual interests causing distress/impairment or entailing harm/risk to others (or nonconsenting persons).

- **Nonpathological atypical sexual interest** — The interest does not cause distress/impairment or harm to others, so no disorder is diagnosed.
- **Manic Episode / Substance Intoxication** (Table 3.3.1) — Hypersexual or atypical behavior occurs only during mood elevation or intoxication.
- **Neurocognitive Disorder** (Table 3.16.2) — Disinhibited sexual behavior results from cognitive decline.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder** (Table 3.6.1) — Sexual intrusive thoughts are ego-dystonic obsessions, not paraphilic arousal.
- **Conduct Disorder / Antisocial Personality Disorder** (Table 3.17.4) — Sexual offenses occur within a broader antisocial pattern rather than a specific paraphilic interest.
