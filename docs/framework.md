# The Six-Step Differential Diagnosis Method

> Before working through a symptom decision tree, orient every case with this ordered method. The steps move from the most fundamental questions about the validity and etiology of the presentation toward the specific primary disorder and, finally, the boundary with normality. Each step should be considered in order because an earlier step can render later ones moot (for example, a substance etiology reframes the entire differential).

## Step 1: Rule out feigning (Malingering and Factitious Disorder)

**Confirm the symptoms are reported in good faith before anything else — if the presentation is fabricated or exaggerated, no downstream diagnosis is reliable.**

Malingering and Factitious Disorder both involve intentionally produced or feigned symptoms; they are separated by motivation. Malingering is driven by an external, tangible incentive (compensation, avoiding work or legal consequences, obtaining medications or shelter). In Factitious Disorder the deception continues even without an obvious external reward. Raise suspicion when there is a clear secondary gain, when the picture matches a lay stereotype of illness rather than a recognized syndrome, when the story shifts markedly between encounters, when the presentation mirrors a role model, or when the patient is notably manipulative or suggestible. This step is context-dependent — it matters most in forensic, disability, and correctional settings — which is why it is not repeated inside every decision tree.

Raise your index of suspicion when:

- Clear external incentive tied to being diagnosed (disability, forensic, prison)
- Symptom cluster resembles a lay notion of mental illness more than a real syndrome
- Presentation changes radically between encounters
- Presentation mimics a nearby role model (another patient, an ill relative)
- Patient is characteristically manipulative or highly suggestible

## Step 2: Rule out a substance / medication etiology

**Ask first whether a substance, medication, or toxin acting on the CNS is producing the symptoms — the single most commonly missed cause in practice.**

Almost any psychiatric presentation can be produced by a substance. First establish whether the person is using anything (history, collateral, exam, and toxicology, since intake is routinely underestimated). Then decide the relationship: (a) the substance directly causes the symptoms → a Substance/Medication-Induced Disorder, Intoxication, or Withdrawal; (b) the substance use is a consequence or associated feature of a primary disorder (e.g., self-medication); or (c) the two are independent. Attribute symptoms to a substance when there is a plausible temporal link, when the amount and duration are sufficient to cause that symptom, and when no better non-substance explanation exists. Persistence well beyond intoxication/withdrawal (roughly one month of abstinence is the DSM-5 guideline, applied flexibly) points toward a primary disorder — with the noted exceptions of substance-induced neurocognitive disorder and hallucinogen persisting perception disorder.

Raise your index of suspicion when:

- Is the person using any drug of abuse, medication, or toxin?
- Is there a close temporal relationship between use and symptom onset/maintenance?
- Is the amount and duration of use sufficient to cause this symptom?
- Do symptoms persist during a sustained period of abstinence (points to primary disorder)?
- Is there a strong alternative explanation (family history, prior episodes off substances)?

## Step 3: Rule out a disorder due to another medical condition

**Determine whether a nonpsychiatric medical condition is directly producing the symptoms via its physiological effect on the brain.**

Virtually any presentation can be the direct physiological consequence of a general medical condition (e.g., depressive disorder due to hypothyroidism, psychosis due to a CNS lesion). Two clues raise the index of suspicion: (1) a temporal pattern in which the psychiatric symptoms follow the onset of the medical condition, vary with its severity, and remit when it resolves; and (2) an atypical presentation — unusual age at onset, atypical symptom pattern, or atypical course (e.g., first manic episode in an elderly patient, severe cognitive or weight loss out of proportion to a mild mood disturbance). Neither clue is infallible; symptoms can be the first harbinger of a medical illness or a late complication. Direct the workup toward conditions that are both common and plausible for the presenting symptom rather than screening indiscriminately.

Raise your index of suspicion when:

- Do symptoms begin after, track with, and remit alongside a medical condition?
- Is the presentation atypical in symptom pattern, age at onset, or course?
- Are there physical exam or laboratory findings pointing to an organic cause?
- Could a medication for the medical condition be responsible (loops back to Step 2)?

## Step 4: Determine the specific primary disorder(s)

**With substances and medical causes excluded, identify which primary DSM-5 disorder(s) best account for the presentation.**

This is where the symptom decision trees do most of their work. DSM-5 groups disorders around shared presenting symptoms precisely to structure this differential. Follow the relevant tree from the presenting symptom through its decision points. Because comorbidity is common and diagnoses are frequently not mutually exclusive, more than one tree may apply, and a single tree may need to be traversed more than once to capture co-occurring conditions. After reaching a candidate diagnosis, review its differential table (Chapter 3) to confirm the main competitors have been considered and ruled out.

## Step 5: Differentiate Adjustment Disorder from residual Other Specified / Unspecified categories

**For clinically significant presentations that don't meet a specific disorder's threshold, decide between an Adjustment Disorder and a residual category.**

Many real presentations fall below the symptom count, severity, or duration required for a specific diagnosis yet still cause clinically significant distress or impairment and reflect a genuine dysfunction. If the symptoms are a maladaptive response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor, diagnose an Adjustment Disorder — the key judgment is whether the stressor is etiological, not merely present. Otherwise, use the Other Specified or Unspecified category of the grouping that best fits the symptoms. Choose Other Specified when you wish to record the specific reason the presentation falls short; choose Unspecified when you decline to specify (insufficient information, or a clinical reason not to).

## Step 6: Establish the boundary with no mental disorder

**Confirm the presentation actually crosses the threshold into disorder — clinically significant distress or impairment reflecting an internal dysfunction.**

Most DSM-5 criteria sets require that the disturbance cause clinically significant distress or impairment in functioning. Many individual symptoms (transient anxiety, low mood, insomnia, sexual difficulty) are part of ordinary experience and are not, by themselves, a disorder. 'Clinically significant' is deliberately left to clinical judgment and is shaped by culture, setting, and available resources; in help-seeking clinical populations the threshold is usually already met. Finally, some impairing but normative reactions (e.g., uncomplicated bereavement) do not represent an internal dysfunction and are coded, if at all, under conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention rather than as a mental disorder.

## A note on comorbidity

Differential diagnosis usually means choosing one best explanation from mutually exclusive contenders, but DSM-5 diagnoses are frequently not mutually exclusive. Assigning more than one diagnosis is often necessary to describe a presentation fully, and comorbid conditions can relate to one another in several ways (one causing another, a shared underlying cause, artificial splitting of one syndrome, definitional overlap, or chance co-occurrence). Multiple diagnoses do not imply multiple independent disease processes — treat DSM-5 diagnoses as descriptive building blocks for communication.
